# Respiratory Systems Review

## The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

Follow the air down the tract, then step outward through the anatomy, then zoom out to the sinister and the contextual:

> **AIRWAY → AIR SACS → LINING → SINISTER → CONTEXT**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Airway** | Larynx to bronchioles | Cough, sputum, wheeze, hoarseness |
| **Air sacs** | Alveoli / gas exchange | Dyspnoea + exercise tolerance |
| **Lining** | Pleura and chest wall | Pleuritic chest pain |
| **Sinister** | The must-not-miss cluster | Haemoptysis, fever/night sweats, weight loss |
| **Context** | The exposures that cause lung disease | Smoking, occupation, pets/travel, TB contact, snoring/OSA |

The airway chunk flows logically: air moving through a diseased tube makes noise (wheeze), irritates it (cough), and the tube produces secretions (sputum). Say "airway, air sacs, lining, sinister, context" and the ~11 questions rebuild themselves.

## The questions

### 1. Cough — *"Do you have a cough? How long has it lasted? Worse at any particular time?"*

**Why we ask:** Duration and pattern do most of the diagnostic work. The single most important split is acute (<3 weeks) vs chronic (>8 weeks) — acute is usually infective; chronic demands an explanation.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How long have you had it?"* — Under 3 weeks → usually infective · over 8 weeks → chronic cough, needs a systematic cause
- *"Do you cough anything up? How much a day — a teaspoon, an eggcup, a cupful? What colour?"* — Purulent with fever → infection · cupfuls, worse with position → bronchiectasis · mucoid most mornings in a smoker → chronic bronchitis
- **!** *"Any blood in it, even streaks?"* — Any haemoptysis needs an explanation — cancer, TB and PE all sit here
- *"Is it worse at any particular time — at night, or first thing in the morning?"* — Night → asthma or reflux · early morning and productive → chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis
- *"Any wheeze or tightness in the chest with it?"* — Points to obstruction — asthma or COPD
- *"Any fever, chills or night sweats? Have you become short of breath?"* — Fever → infective · new breathlessness → parenchymal disease, not just airway irritation
- **!** *"Do you smoke or vape? Has a long-standing cough changed recently?"* — A new or changed cough in a smoker → exclude lung cancer
- *"Any new tablets, especially for blood pressure? Any heartburn, or a blocked, dripping nose?"* — ACE inhibitor, reflux and post-nasal drip — the three commonest causes of a chronic dry cough, all missed if you don't ask

> Full work-up → **cough-haemoptysis** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Chronic productive cough, most days for 3 months in 2 consecutive years** → rules in chronic bronchitis (COPD) by definition
- **Dry nocturnal cough, with wheeze, worse with exercise/cold air/allergens** → asthma (nocturnal symptoms = poor control)
- **Dry cough on an ACE inhibitor** → drug cause; check the medication list before ordering anything
- **Bovine (hollow, non-explosive) cough with hoarseness** → recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy (vocal cords can't close); note a barking cough is croup/tracheomalacia and a brassy cough suggests tracheal compression
- **Postural cough with large sputum volumes** → bronchiectasis
- **Cough with reflux symptoms or postnasal drip** → the two great non-pulmonary causes of chronic cough

**Red flag:** New or changed cough >3 weeks in a smoker → exclude lung cancer.

### 2. Sputum — *"Do you cough anything up? How much a day — a teaspoon, an eggcup? What colour?"*

**Why we ask:** Volume and character localise the pathology. Patients rarely volunteer this — you must ask, and quantify in household measures.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How much do you cough up in a day — a teaspoon, an eggcup, a cupful?"* — Quantify in household measures: cupfuls daily, especially worse with position, is the volume that rules in bronchiectasis
- *"What colour is it?"* — Yellow/green and purulent → infective · clear/white (mucoid) → chronic bronchitis or asthma · rusty → classic (if now rare) for pneumococcal pneumonia
- *"Does it smell foul?"* — Foul-smelling, dark-coloured sputum → lung abscess with anaerobic organisms
- *"Is there any blood in it?"* — Any streak of blood needs its own work-up — see the haemoptysis question
- *"Is it frothy and pink rather than a true sputum?"* — Pink frothy secretions are not sputum at all → pulmonary oedema, a cardiac answer hiding in a respiratory question
- *"Worse in the mornings, or worse lying on one side?"* — Morning-predominant in a smoker → chronic bronchitis · postural pooling → bronchiectasis
- *"Any fever with it?"* — Fever + purulent sputum → acute infective exacerbation or pneumonia

> Full work-up → **cough-haemoptysis** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Purulent (yellow/green), acute, with fever** → infective (bronchitis/pneumonia)
- **Large volumes daily ("cupfuls"), especially postural** → rules in bronchiectasis
- **Mucoid, chronic, worse in the mornings, in a smoker** → chronic bronchitis
- **Foul-smelling, dark-coloured sputum** → lung abscess with anaerobic organisms
- **Pink and frothy** → pulmonary oedema — a cardiac answer hiding in a respiratory question
- **Rusty sputum** → classic (if now uncommon) for pneumococcal pneumonia

### 3. Wheeze — *"Do you ever hear a whistling sound when you breathe?"*

**Why we ask:** Wheeze = airflow through narrowed lower airways, predominantly expiratory. It establishes obstructive physiology and, with pattern, splits asthma from COPD.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Does it come and go, or is it there all the time?"* — Episodic, variable → asthma · fixed and persistent → COPD
- *"What brings it on — exercise, cold air, allergens, colds?"* — Classic asthma triggers; also ask about hay fever and eczema (atopy)
- *"Is it worse at night or first thing in the morning?"* — Nocturnal or early-morning wheeze is a marker of poorly controlled asthma
- **!** *"Is the noise loudest in your throat or neck, and is it worse breathing in or out?"* — Loudest over the trachea and worse on inspiration = stridor, not wheeze — upper airway obstruction, an emergency, not asthma/COPD
- **!** *"Is it all over the chest, or in one spot, and is it a single note or many?"* — New, localised, single-pitch (monophonic) wheeze in a smoker → think an obstructing tumour or foreign body, not diffuse airway disease

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Episodic wheeze, diurnal variation (worse at night/early morning), triggers (exercise, cold, allergens, viral URTIs), atopy** → rules in asthma
- **Fixed wheeze with chronic dyspnoea in a heavy smoker** → COPD
- **Stridor — harsh, inspiratory, heard loudest over the trachea** → upper airway obstruction (tumour, foreign body, anaphylaxis) — do not file this as wheeze; it's an emergency
- **New localised/monophonic wheeze in a smoker** → single obstructed bronchus — think tumour or foreign body

### 4. Hoarseness — *"Has your voice changed or become hoarse?"*

**Why we ask:** A sneaky but crucial screen: the left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under the aortic arch through the chest, so a left hilar/mediastinal lung cancer can present as a voice change (apical/Pancoast tumours instead cause Horner's syndrome and brachial-plexus signs). Persistent hoarseness >3 weeks needs laryngoscopy.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"How long has your voice been like this?"* — Days–weeks after a cold → laryngitis, benign · persistent beyond 3 weeks needs laryngoscopy
- **!** *"Do you smoke? Any cough, or coughing up blood, with it?"* — Hoarseness + smoking + cough/haemoptysis → raises lung cancer (via recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement) or laryngeal cancer
- *"Do you use a steroid inhaler, and how do you use it?"* — Hoarse voice in a heavy inhaled-corticosteroid user → local steroid effect or oral candidiasis — check inhaler technique and spacer use

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Hoarseness + smoking + cough/haemoptysis** → rules in possible lung cancer or laryngeal cancer
- **Hoarseness after a URTI, resolving in days–weeks** → laryngitis — benign
- **Hoarse voice in a heavy inhaled-corticosteroid user** → local steroid effect/candida — check inhaler technique

**Red flag:** Persistent hoarseness >3 weeks needs laryngoscopy.

### 5. Dyspnoea + exercise tolerance — *"Do you get short of breath? How far on the flat? Compared with a year ago?"*

**Why we ask:** The gas-exchange question. As with cardio, quantify exercise tolerance and establish trajectory. The tempo of onset is the key discriminator.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How long have you been short of breath — did it come on suddenly, over hours, or gradually over months?"* — Seconds–minutes → PE, pneumothorax, anaphylaxis, foreign body · hours–days → infection, asthma exacerbation, effusion · months–years → COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, anaemia, deconditioning
- *"How far can you walk on the flat, and how many stairs, before it stops you? How does that compare with a year ago?"* — Quantify in blocks/stairs and track the trajectory — more reliable than the patient's own grading of severity
- **!** *"Do you ever wake at night breathless, or need to sleep propped up on pillows?"* — Orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea point to left ventricular failure, not primary lung disease
- *"Any fever with it?"* — Fever → infective cause — pneumonia or an infective exacerbation
- *"Do you smoke?"* — Sets pre-test probability for COPD and lung cancer
- **!** *"Any tightness in the chest when you're breathless?"* — Tightness → angina or asthma, depending on the rest of the story
- *"Any wheeze or cough with it?"* — Points to airways disease — asthma or COPD
- *"Does it feel like you can't get a satisfying breath in, with tingling round your lips or fingers?"* — Suggests hyperventilation/anxiety — a diagnosis of exclusion once organic causes are covered

> Full work-up → **breathlessness** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- Minutes: pneumothorax, PE, foreign body, anaphylaxis, flash pulmonary oedema
- Hours–days: pneumonia, asthma exacerbation, effusion accumulating
- Months–years: COPD, pulmonary fibrosis (progressive, insidious), also anaemia and deconditioning
- **Episodic/variable with well periods between** → asthma; fixed and progressive → COPD/fibrosis
- Use the cardiac-vs-respiratory discriminator table in the cardio file — orthopnoea/PND/oedema pull cardiac; wheeze/sputum/smoking pull respiratory
- **Wakes at night breathless, or needs to sleep propped on pillows** → orthopnoea/PND, pulling toward cardiac failure rather than primary lung disease
- **Sensation of not getting a satisfying breath in, with perioral/finger tingling** → hyperventilation/anxiety, a diagnosis of exclusion

**Red flag:** Dyspnoea at rest, rapid progression, dyspnoea with stridor (upper airway obstruction — an emergency, not a wheeze).

### 6. Pleuritic chest pain — *"Any sharp chest pain when you breathe in or cough?"*

**Why we ask:** The lung itself is insensate — respiratory chest pain means pleural or chest wall involvement. Character separates it from cardiac pain.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Where exactly is the pain, and does it stay in one spot?"* — Localised, one-sided pain points to a pleural rather than a cardiac source
- *"Is it worse breathing in, or coughing?"* — Confirms a pleuritic pattern — pleura or chest wall, not the (insensate) lung itself
- **!** *"Did it come on suddenly, and are you short of breath with it?"* — Sudden pleuritic pain + acute dyspnoea in a tall, thin, young smoker → spontaneous pneumothorax
- **!** *"Any swelling or pain in a calf, recent surgery, a long flight or immobility?"* — Pleuritic pain + calf swelling/recent immobility → pulmonary embolism until excluded
- *"Does pressing on the area, or twisting your chest, reproduce it?"* — Reproducible on palpation points to a musculoskeletal cause — but only after PE, pneumothorax and pneumonia are excluded

> Full work-up → **chest-pain** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Sharp, localised, worse on inspiration/coughing** → pleuritic: PE, pneumonia reaching the pleura, pneumothorax, viral pleurisy
- **Sudden pleuritic pain + acute dyspnoea in a tall thin young smoker** → spontaneous pneumothorax
- **Pleuritic pain + calf swelling/recent immobility** → PE
- **Reproduced by palpation or twisting** → chest wall/MSK (but exclude the above first)
- **Central, tight, exertional** → back to the cardiac pathway

### 7. Haemoptysis — *"Have you ever coughed up blood, even streaks?"*

**Why we ask:** This is the highest-stakes question in the respiratory screen. Any haemoptysis needs an explanation; the differential includes three killers.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Are you sure it's coughed up, not vomited, or from your nose or gums?"* — Distinguishes true haemoptysis from haematemesis (follows nausea/retching, mixed with vomit) and nasopharyngeal bleeding (appears in the mouth, not mixed with sputum)
- **!** *"How much — streaks, a teaspoon, or much more? How often?"* — Streaks/under 20 mL in 24h = mild · over 250 mL in 24h, or rapid, = massive haemoptysis, a medical emergency
- **!** *"Do you smoke, and have you lost weight?"* — Haemoptysis + smoking + weight loss → lung cancer until excluded
- **!** *"Any fever or drenching night sweats? Any TB contact or travel?"* — Fever, night sweats ± TB contact/travel → tuberculosis
- **!** *"Did it come on suddenly, with breathlessness or sharp chest pain? Any recent surgery, long flight or immobility?"* — Sudden haemoptysis + pleuritic pain + dyspnoea ± immobility → pulmonary embolism
- *"Is it streaked through infected, purulent sputum?"* — Blood-streaked purulent sputum during an infective flare is usually the common, benign end of the spectrum — infection or bronchiectasis
- *"Any past TB, bronchiectasis or cystic fibrosis?"* — Known structural lung disease makes recurrent haemoptysis more likely from that same source — but still work it up fresh each time

> Full work-up → **cough-haemoptysis** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Haemoptysis + weight loss + smoking history** → lung cancer until excluded
- **Haemoptysis + fever + night sweats ± TB contact/travel** → tuberculosis
- **Sudden haemoptysis + pleuritic pain + dyspnoea ± recent surgery/immobility** → pulmonary embolism
- **Blood-streaked purulent sputum during an infective exacerbation** → usually infection/bronchiectasis — the common benign end of the spectrum
- Confirm it's coughed, not vomited (haematemesis) or from the nose/gums — patients confuse these
- **Rapid, or over 250 mL in 24 hours** → massive haemoptysis: a medical emergency needing immediate escalation, not just outpatient work-up

**Red flag:** The whole question is a red flag — never omit it, never ignore a positive.

### 8. Fever, night sweats — *"Any fevers, shivers or drenching night sweats?"*

**Why we ask:** Marks the process as infective or inflammatory, and drenching night sweats specifically flag the chronic granulomatous and malignant causes.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it a fever with shivers/rigors, or drenching sweats that soak the sheets at night?"* — Rigors point to bacteraemia/acute infection — drenching night sweats (changing the sheets) is the specific pattern that flags TB and lymphoma, not just feeling hot at night
- *"How long has this been going on?"* — Acute, short prodrome → bacterial pneumonia · longer prodrome over days → viral · weeks, with weight loss → TB, lymphoma
- *"Any cough, sputum or chest pain with it?"* — Fever + productive cough ± pleuritic pain → pneumonia
- **!** *"Any weight loss, or contact with TB, or recent travel?"* — Adds to the TB/lymphoma picture — pursue with the weight-loss and exposure questions
- *"Do you have a chronic lung condition like bronchiectasis? Are these fevers low-grade and recurring?"* — Recurrent low-grade fevers with copious sputum in a known bronchiectatic → infective exacerbation

> Full work-up → **fever** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Acute fever + productive cough ± pleuritic pain** → pneumonia
- **Drenching night sweats (change the sheets) + weight loss + chronic cough** → TB or lymphoma — pair this with the travel/contact questions
- **Recurrent low-grade fevers with copious sputum** → bronchiectasis exacerbations

### 9. Weight loss — *"Have you lost weight without trying to? How much, and over how long?"*

**Why we ask:** Unintentional weight loss reframes a respiratory symptom as systemic disease. Paired with cough, haemoptysis or night sweats, it moves malignancy and TB to the top of the differential.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How much weight, and over how long?"* — Quantify in kg and timeframe — unintentional loss of >5% body weight over 6–12 months is what makes this significant
- *"Is it definitely unintentional — appetite, diet and activity unchanged?"* — Confirms it's not simply reduced intake or increased exercise before chasing an organic cause
- **!** *"Any cough, haemoptysis or smoking history?"* — Weight loss + haemoptysis + smoking → lung cancer until excluded
- **!** *"Any night sweats, or TB contact/travel?"* — Weight loss + drenching night sweats + chronic cough ± TB contact/travel → TB or lymphoma
- *"Any reason you might be eating less — appetite, swallowing, mood?"* — Weight loss explained by reduced intake with no chest symptoms points outside the respiratory system entirely

> Full work-up → **weight-loss** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Weight loss + haemoptysis + smoking history** → lung cancer until excluded
- **Weight loss + drenching night sweats + chronic cough ± TB contact/travel** → TB or lymphoma
- **Weight loss explained by reduced intake (anorexia, dysphagia, low mood) with no chest symptoms** → look outside the respiratory system first

**Red flag:** Unintentional weight loss with any respiratory symptom is a red flag — quantify (kg and timeframe) and pursue to a cause.

### 10. Exposure screen — *"Do you smoke, or ever? What work have you done over your life? Pets, birds, recent travel, TB contact?"*

**Why we ask:** The lung is the organ most exposed to the environment — a respiratory history without an exposure history is half a history. This sets pre-test probability just as the risk-factor screen does in cardio.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How many cigarettes or packs a day, and for how many years — or when did you quit?"* — Quantify in pack-years (packs/day × years) — the single most useful number for COPD and lung cancer risk
- *"What jobs have you done across your whole working life, not just now?"* — Ask lifetime exposure — asbestos disease and silicosis can surface 20–40 years after exposure ended
- **!** *"Any work with stone benchtops, mining, quarrying, construction, shipyards or insulation?"* — Engineered/artificial stone cutting → silicosis (Australia's current high-yield exposure, product banned 2024) · asbestos trades → asbestosis/mesothelioma, latency up to 30 years
- *"Any pets or birds at home, or work with animals?"* — Bird fancier's lung, psittacosis, Q fever
- *"Any recent travel, or contact with someone with TB?"* — Sets pre-test probability for TB and endemic mycoses by geography
- *"Do your symptoms get better on holidays or over the weekend, away from work?"* — Improvement away from work is the classic clue to occupational asthma — commonly from spray paints, flour dust or soldering fumes
- *"Any medications — amiodarone, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin, ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers?"* — Amiodarone/methotrexate/nitrofurantoin → pulmonary fibrosis · ACE inhibitor → cough · beta-blocker → bronchospasm

**Rules in / rules out:**
- Smoking: quantify in pack-years (packs/day × years); never smoked / ex (when quit) / current
- **Occupation — lifetime, not just current (asbestos disease often appears decades later, commonly 20–40+ years): mining/quarrying and engineered/artificial-stone benchtop work (silicosis — the current high-yield Australian exposure; the product was banned in Australia in 2024), construction/shipyards/insulation (asbestos** → mesothelioma), farming (hypersensitivity pneumonitis), baking/spray-painting (occupational asthma — ask if symptoms improve on holidays)
- Pets/birds: bird fancier's lung, psittacosis
- Travel/TB contact: TB, but also endemic mycoses depending on geography
- Medications: amiodarone, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin (pulmonary fibrosis); ACE inhibitor (cough); beta-blockers (bronchospasm)

### 11. Snoring / daytime sleepiness — *"Do you snore heavily? Has anyone seen you stop breathing in your sleep? Do you fall asleep by day — even driving?"*

**Why we ask:** Obstructive sleep apnoea is common, underdiagnosed, and dangerous (motor vehicle accidents, resistant hypertension, AF). The best historian is the bed partner.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"Has anyone seen you stop breathing, choke or gasp in your sleep?"* — Witnessed apnoea/choking is a more reliable sign of OSA than snoring alone — snoring is common without OSA
- **!** *"Do you wake unrefreshed, or fall asleep during the day — reading, watching TV, or driving?"* — Daytime somnolence, especially at the wheel, is what makes this dangerous (motor vehicle accidents) and worth a sleep study
- *"Are you waking breathless and needing to sit up, or is it more that you're gasping/snoring through the night?"* — Distinguishes OSA from paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (cardiac failure) — PND wakes the patient gasping with other symptoms of failure, not snoring
- *"Any high blood pressure that's hard to control, or an irregular heartbeat?"* — OSA is linked to resistant hypertension and atrial fibrillation — worth flagging even if sleep itself isn't the presenting complaint

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Loud snoring + witnessed apnoeas + unrefreshing sleep + daytime somnolence (especially while driving)** → rules in OSA — refer for a sleep study
- Distinguish from PND (wakes gasping but from pulmonary oedema, with other failure symptoms) and simple poor sleep hygiene

## Discriminator table: the chronic breathless patient

| Clue | Asthma | COPD | Pulmonary fibrosis | Cardiac failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Often young, atopic | >40, smoker | Insidious, older | Any; IHD/HTN history |
| Pattern | Episodic, variable, nocturnal | Fixed, slowly progressive | Relentlessly progressive | Progressive ± acute decompensation |
| Cough | Dry, nocturnal | Productive, morning | Dry | Dry, nocturnal, ± frothy sputum |
| Wheeze | Prominent | Common | Absent | "Cardiac wheeze" possible |
| Key positives | Triggers, atopy, hay fever | Pack-years | Occupational/drug exposure | Orthopnoea, PND, oedema |

## Quick-recall list (drill version)

AIRWAY: cough, sputum (volume + colour), wheeze, hoarseness · AIR SACS: dyspnoea + exercise tolerance · LINING: pleuritic chest pain · SINISTER: haemoptysis, fever/night sweats, weight loss · CONTEXT: smoking (pack-years), occupation, pets/travel/TB contact, snoring/daytime sleepiness
