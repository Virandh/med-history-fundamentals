# Neurological Systems Review

## The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

Work from the top of the neuraxis down, then out to the special senses:

> **BRAIN → CRANIAL → CORD/NERVE → EPISODES → HEAD**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Brain** | Cortex / global | Confusion, memory, speech |
| **Cranial** | Cranial nerves | Vision, hearing, swallowing, facial weakness, smell |
| **Cord/nerve** | Motor & sensory tracts | Weakness, numbness/tingling, coordination/balance, gait |
| **Episodes** | Paroxysmal | Blackouts, seizures, dizziness/vertigo |
| **Head** | Meninges/vessels | Headache |

The single most useful discriminator running through the whole system is the tempo of onset: sudden (seconds–minutes) → vascular (stroke, haemorrhage, SAH); subacute (days–weeks) → inflammatory/infective/compressive; chronic (months–years) → degenerative/neoplastic.

## The questions

### 1. Confusion / delirium — *"Has anyone noticed you seem more confused than usual — coming and going, or constant?"*

**Why we ask:** Fluctuating confusion over hours–days is delirium until an underlying systemic cause is found; a gradual, non-fluctuating decline points instead to dementia.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Did this come on suddenly, over hours to days, and does it come and go through the day?"* — Acute, fluctuating, often worse at night → delirium; gradual and steady over months → dementia instead
- *"Any fever, new medications, or recent illness?"* — Delirium always has an underlying trigger — infection, drugs, or a metabolic disturbance — go looking for it rather than treating confusion as the diagnosis
- *"Has anyone else noticed this — family, carers?"* — A collateral history is often more reliable than the patient's own account, especially with impaired insight

> Full work-up → **confusion-delirium** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Acute confusion over hours–days, fluctuating, ± reversed sleep–wake** → delirium — look for the systemic cause (infection, drugs, metabolic)

---

### 2. Memory — *"Any problems with your memory?"*

**Why we ask:** Progressive memory loss with functional decline in an older adult raises dementia; get a collateral history, since patients often underreport it.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it more that you forget recent things, or old memories too?"* — Recent memory loss with preserved distant memory is the typical early pattern of most dementias
- *"Is it affecting daily activities — finances, medications, driving?"* — Functional decline alongside memory loss is what separates dementia from benign age-related forgetfulness
- *"Has anyone else noticed a change, and how has it progressed?"* — Collateral history is essential — patients with dementia often underreport their own memory loss

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Progressive memory loss + functional decline in the elderly** → dementia (get collateral history)

---

### 3. Speech — *"Any trouble finding words, or getting words out clearly?"*

**Why we ask:** Dysphasia (word-finding/language) localises to the dominant hemisphere; dysarthria (slurred articulation) localises to the cerebellum, bulbar muscles or their nerve supply — the distinction matters for localisation.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"Did this come on suddenly, or has it developed gradually?"* — Sudden → stroke/TIA until excluded; gradual in an older adult → consider primary progressive aphasia or early dementia
- *"Is it that you can't find the right words, or that the words come out slurred?"* — Word-finding difficulty (dysphasia) localises to the dominant hemisphere; slurred articulation (dysarthria) localises to the cerebellum, bulbar muscles, or their nerve supply
- **!** *"Any weakness, numbness, or facial droop alongside it?"* — Any accompanying focal deficit strengthens a stroke/TIA localisation

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Sudden word-finding difficulty or slurred speech** → stroke/TIA until excluded
- **Gradual word-finding difficulty in an older adult** → consider primary progressive aphasia / early dementia

---

### 4. Vision (neuro screen) — *"Any change in your vision, or a curtain/shadow across it?"*

**Why we ask:** Sudden painless monocular loss in the amaurosis fugax pattern is a TIA warning — cross-reference the ENT & Eyes visual-loss entry for the full differential.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"Did it come on suddenly, like a curtain coming down over one eye?"* — Sudden painless monocular visual loss in this pattern is amaurosis fugax — a TIA warning
- *"Was it one eye, or both? Has it come back, or is it still there?"* — Monocular points to the eye or optic nerve; transient and resolved is the classic TIA-warning pattern

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Sudden painless monocular visual loss ("curtain")** → amaurosis fugax / retinal artery occlusion — a TIA warning

---

### 5. Hearing (neuro screen) — *"Any change in your hearing?"*

**Why we ask:** New unilateral hearing loss with other cranial nerve signs raises a cerebellopontine angle lesion — cross-reference the ENT & Eyes hearing-loss entry.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it one side or both, and did it come on suddenly or gradually?"* — Sudden unilateral hearing loss is an otological emergency in its own right; gradual unilateral loss raises a cerebellopontine angle lesion
- *"Any facial numbness, weakness, or unsteadiness alongside it?"* — Combined with other cranial nerve signs or ataxia, consider a cerebellopontine angle lesion (e.g. acoustic neuroma)

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Unilateral hearing loss + facial numbness/weakness or ataxia** → consider a cerebellopontine angle lesion (e.g. acoustic neuroma)

---

### 6. Smell — *"Any change in your sense of smell?"*

**Why we ask:** Isolated anosmia can be an early non-motor feature of Parkinson's disease, or reflect a frontal skull-base lesion pressing on the olfactory tract.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it just smell, or has your sense of taste changed too? Any nasal congestion or history of head injury?"* — Excludes the common local causes (rhinosinusitis, prior head trauma) before considering a neurodegenerative cause

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Isolated anosmia, older adult, no nasal cause** → consider a neurodegenerative cause

---

### 7. Facial weakness — *"Any weakness or drooping of the face?"*

**Why we ask:** Sparing of the forehead localises an upper motor neuron (central, e.g. stroke) lesion; forehead involvement means a lower motor neuron (peripheral) lesion — the single most useful localiser in facial weakness.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Can you raise your eyebrows and close your eyes tightly on both sides?"* — Forehead spared (can still raise eyebrows) → UMN pattern (stroke); whole side including the forehead → LMN pattern (Bell's palsy)
- **!** *"Did it come on suddenly, with any other weakness or speech trouble?"* — Sudden facial weakness with other focal signs → stroke until excluded
- *"Any ear pain, rash near the ear, or a lump in front of the ear?"* — Ear pain/vesicular rash → Ramsay Hunt syndrome; a parotid lump → tumour infiltrating the nerve, not Bell's palsy

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Forehead spared, lower face only** → UMN (stroke) — usually with other focal signs
- **Whole side of the face including the forehead** → LMN (Bell's palsy; exclude Ramsay Hunt or a parotid tumour)

**Red flag:** Sudden facial weakness with other focal neuro signs → stroke until excluded.

---

### 8. Diplopia (neuro screen) — *"Any double vision?"*

**Why we ask:** Diplopia localises a cranial nerve palsy (III, IV, VI) — think microvascular, compressive (aneurysm), or raised ICP.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is the double vision side-by-side, or up-and-down? Does it go away if you cover one eye?"* — Resolves with one eye covered → true binocular diplopia from a cranial nerve palsy (III, IV, VI), not an ocular/refractive problem
- **!** *"Any eyelid droop, or is one pupil bigger than the other? Any pain?"* — Painful third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil → compressive aneurysm, a neurosurgical emergency
- *"Did it come on suddenly, and do you have diabetes or vascular risk factors?"* — Sudden onset with vascular risk factors supports a microvascular (diabetic) cause — usually pupil-sparing, unlike a compressive lesion

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **** → cranial nerve palsy (III, IV, VI) — microvascular, compressive (aneurysm), or raised ICP

**Red flag:** Painful third nerve palsy with a fixed dilated pupil → compressive aneurysm, a neurosurgical emergency.

---

### 9. Weakness — *"Any weakness in the arms or legs — one side or both? Sudden or gradual?"*

**Why we ask:** Distribution + tempo localise the lesion along the neuraxis.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How quickly did it come on — seconds, over days, or gradually over months?"* — Sudden (seconds–minutes) → vascular (stroke/TIA) · subacute (days) → inflammatory/infective (Guillain–Barré, myasthenia) · gradual (months) → myopathy or a compressive lesion
- **!** *"Is it one side of the body, or both? Does it involve the face as well?"* — One-sided face/arm/leg → stroke/TIA — a time-critical red flag
- *"Has it improved, or is it still there?"* — Resolution within 24 hours → TIA; persisting beyond that → stroke, with imaging changes to match
- *"Any trouble with speech or swallowing alongside it?"* — Cortical/brainstem involvement — reinforces a stroke localisation
- *"Is it worse with repeated use through the day, with drooping eyelids or double vision?"* — Fatigable weakness plus ptosis/diplopia → myasthenia gravis
- **!** *"Any pins and needles, back pain, or bladder/bowel symptoms with it?"* — Weakness + a sensory level + bladder involvement → cord compression, a surgical emergency
- *"Do you have high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, or a smoking history?"* — Sets the pre-test probability for a vascular cause and shapes secondary prevention
- *"Are you on blood thinners, or do you have a history of a heart valve problem or fevers?"* — Anticoagulation changes the bleed-vs-clot calculus; endocarditis is an embolic source worth excluding

> Full work-up → **weakness** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Sudden, one-sided (face/arm/leg) ± speech** → stroke/TIA — a time-critical red flag
- **Ascending symmetrical weakness over days ± after infection** → Guillain–Barré
- **Proximal, symmetrical, gradual (difficulty with stairs/rising/hair)** → myopathy
- **Weakness worse with use, better with rest, ± ptosis/diplopia** → myasthenia

**Red flag:** Weakness + a sensory level + bladder involvement → cord compression.

---

### 10. Sensory disturbance — *"Any numbness, pins and needles or altered sensation? Where?"*

**Why we ask:** The distribution maps the lesion (nerve, root, cord, or brain).

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Where exactly do you feel it — can you show me the boundary?"* — Distribution maps the lesion: glove-and-stocking → peripheral neuropathy · a single nerve territory → entrapment · a dermatomal band → nerve root · a level on the trunk → spinal cord
- **!** *"Did it come on suddenly, with any weakness alongside it?"* — Sudden, one-sided, with weakness → stroke
- *"Is it worse at night, and does shaking your hand or hanging your arm off the bed help?"* — Nocturnal worsening relieved by shaking the hand (the flick sign) is classic for carpal tunnel/median nerve entrapment
- *"Do you have diabetes, drink alcohol, or have any nutritional deficiencies?"* — The common causes of a symmetrical peripheral neuropathy

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Glove-and-stocking** → peripheral neuropathy (diabetes, alcohol, B12)
- **Single nerve territory** → mononeuropathy/entrapment (e.g. median → carpal tunnel)
- **Dermatomal** → nerve root (disc)
- **A sensory level on the trunk** → spinal cord lesion — red flag
- **One-sided, sudden, with weakness** → stroke

### 11. Coordination / gait / falls — *"Any problems with balance, coordination, or walking? Any falls?"*

**Why we ask:** Screens the cerebellum, proprioception and the motor system as an integrated output; falls are a major morbidity in the elderly.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it worse in the dark, or when you close your eyes — like in the shower?"* — Worse without visual input → sensory (proprioceptive) ataxia; unaffected by vision → cerebellar
- *"Any slurred speech, tremor, or clumsiness with your hands as well?"* — Adds cerebellar signs to the picture, rather than a purely proprioceptive or peripheral one
- *"Are your steps small and shuffling, and do your arms swing normally when you walk?"* — Shuffling, stooped, reduced arm swing → parkinsonism
- *"Do you find yourself lifting your foot higher than normal, or tripping on your toes?"* — A high-stepping gait to clear a dropped foot → peripheral nerve (e.g. common peroneal palsy, peripheral neuropathy)
- *"How many falls, and were you injured? What were you doing each time?"* — Circumstances and injury pattern separate a mechanical/gait problem from a blackout — falls are a major cause of morbidity in the elderly and worth quantifying

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Broad-based, clumsy, worse in the dark improving with vision** → sensory ataxia (proprioceptive)
- **Broad-based with slurred speech/tremor, not helped by vision** → cerebellar
- **Shuffling, small steps, stooped, reduced arm swing** → parkinsonism
- **High-stepping (foot drop)** → peripheral nerve

### 12. Sphincter / continence — *"Any new problems with bladder or bowel control?"*

**Why we ask:** New sphincter disturbance with leg symptoms is a spinal-cord red flag — a small question that catches a surgical emergency.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is this new, and does it affect your bladder, your bowels, or both?"* — New sphincter disturbance, especially combined, is what raises concern — long-standing stress incontinence is a different, usually non-neurological, problem
- **!** *"Any numbness around your bottom or inner thighs — like sitting on a saddle?"* — Saddle anaesthesia + sphincter disturbance + leg weakness → cauda equina syndrome, a surgical emergency
- *"Any weakness or numbness in the legs alongside it?"* — Bilateral leg symptoms with sphincter change points to the cord/cauda equina rather than an isolated urological cause
- *"Any back pain that came before this?"* — A preceding disc-type back pain supports a compressive cause at the cord or cauda equina level

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Sphincter disturbance (new urinary retention/incontinence or bowel incontinence) + bilateral leg symptoms + saddle numbness** → cauda equina / cord compression
- **Isolated urge/stress incontinence** → see GU (usually not neurological)

**Red flag:** Sphincter disturbance + saddle anaesthesia + bilateral leg weakness → cauda equina — urgent.

### 13. Blackouts — *"Have you had any episodes of losing consciousness?"*

**Why we ask:** The overlap of syncope (cardiovascular) and seizure (neurological) — the history distinguishes them (cross-reference the cardio syncope entry).

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Did you lose consciousness completely, and for how long?"* — Confirms true loss of consciousness rather than presyncope or a non-syncopal spell
- **!** *"What were you doing right before — standing up quickly, exercising, or passing urine at night?"* — Standing → postural hypotension · exercise → cardiac outflow obstruction, a dangerous one · micturition at night → micturition syncope
- *"Any warning — feeling sick and in a stuffy room, or a strange smell/déjà vu?"* — Nausea in a stuffy room → vasovagal; a strange smell or déjà vu is an aura, and points to seizure instead
- *"Did you bite your tongue, or lose bladder control during it?"* — Supports seizure, though of limited value alone — these can occur in convulsive syncope too
- *"Did anyone see it, and were there jerking movements? Has anyone filmed it?"* — A witness account or video is often the single most useful piece of evidence — jerking makes seizure more likely but can occur with cardiac syncope too
- *"Did you wake up feeling normal, or drowsy and confused?"* — Normal on waking → cardiac syncope; drowsy/confused → seizure
- *"What medications are you on — blood pressure tablets, heart rhythm drugs, or antiepileptics?"* — Antihypertensives and antiarrhythmics can precipitate the episode itself
- **!** *"Has this happened before, and is there a family history of blackouts, sudden death, or epilepsy?"* — Recurrence pattern and family history shift the differential toward an inherited arrhythmia or epilepsy syndrome

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Aura, lateral tongue-bite, post-ictal confusion/drowsiness** → seizure (jerk duration doesn't discriminate: brief clonic jerks occur in convulsive syncope, and prolonged jerking suggests non-epileptic seizures; incontinence and tongue-biting alone are of limited value)
- **Prodromal nausea/greying vision, triggered by standing/pain, rapid clear recovery** → vasovagal syncope
- **Exertional, no warning** → cardiac (see cardio file) — the dangerous one
- Always seek a collateral history from a witness — the patient by definition didn't see it
- **Recurrent episodes with a family history of sudden death or epilepsy** → raises an inherited arrhythmia or epilepsy syndrome

### 14. Seizures — *"Have you ever had a fit or convulsion?"*

**Why we ask:** Distinguishes provoked from unprovoked and localises. Ask about aura (focal onset), and precipitants (alcohol withdrawal, sleep deprivation, flashing lights, missed medication).

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Can you describe exactly what happened, from the very start?"* — A detailed blow-by-blow account is more useful than the label "seizure" — establishes focal vs generalised onset
- *"Did you get any warning beforehand — an odd smell or taste, déjà vu, or a rising feeling in your chest?"* — An aura is itself a focal (aware) seizure and localises the onset
- *"Were you stiff and jerking, or did you just go blank and stare?"* — Tonic–clonic movements → generalised or focal-to-bilateral seizure; blank staring with automatisms (picking, lip-smacking) → focal impaired-awareness seizure
- *"Did you bite your tongue, or lose bladder control?"* — Supports a seizure, though of limited value alone
- *"How did you feel afterwards — confused, drowsy, or any weakness that took a while to settle?"* — Post-ictal drowsiness/confusion supports seizure; transient weakness afterward is Todd's paresis, not a new stroke
- *"Any missed medication, heavy alcohol use or a recent stop, lack of sleep, or flashing lights beforehand?"* — The classic precipitants — worth asking specifically, as patients often don't volunteer them
- *"Has this happened before? How often, and is there a pattern — time of day, during sleep?"* — Recurrence and timing shape the diagnosis of epilepsy versus a single provoked event
- *"Is there a family history of epilepsy, or any past head injury, meningitis, or brain scan?"* — Family history and a structural cause (old injury, past infection) both raise the pre-test probability of a seizure disorder

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Preceding aura** → focal onset
- Precipitants: alcohol withdrawal, sleep deprivation, missed medication

### 15. Dizziness / vertigo — *"When you say dizzy, do you mean the room spins, or more light-headed?"*

**Why we ask:** "Dizziness" splits into two unrelated pathways — force the patient to define it.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"When you say dizzy, do you mean the room is spinning, or more that you feel faint/light-headed?"* — Forces the split between vertigo (vestibular) and presyncope (cardiovascular) — patients are poor at distinguishing these unprompted
- *"Does it happen with a change in head position — rolling over in bed, or looking up?"* — Brief, positional, seconds to minutes → BPPV
- *"How long does each episode last — seconds, hours, or is it constant?"* — Seconds–minutes and positional → BPPV · hours with hearing loss/tinnitus → Ménière's · persistent over 24h without hearing loss → vestibular neuritis
- *"Any hearing loss or ringing in the ears with it?"* — Present suggests a labyrinthine/cochlear process (labyrinthitis, Ménière's, or rarely a stroke affecting the inner ear); absent favours vestibular neuritis or BPPV
- **!** *"Any double vision, slurred speech, weakness, or clumsiness alongside it?"* — Any of these with vertigo points to a central (brainstem/cerebellar) cause — a red flag, not simple peripheral vertigo
- *"Can you still stand and walk during an episode, or does it stop you completely?"* — Able to walk, though unsteady → peripheral; unable to stand or walk at all → more suggestive of a central cause
- *"Any nausea, vomiting, sweating, or headache with it?"* — Common with severe vertigo of either origin, but headache alongside neuro signs raises central causes
- *"Is it only present on standing, or does it happen at rest too?"* — Only on standing → presyncope/orthostatic — back to the cardiovascular pathway, not a primary vestibular problem

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Room spinning (vertigo)** → vestibular: BPPV (brief, positional), vestibular neuritis (acute, sustained), Ménière's (with hearing loss/tinnitus); central causes (brainstem/cerebellar stroke) if with other neuro signs → red flag
- **Light-headed/faint** → presyncope: cardiovascular/orthostatic (back to the cardio pathway)
- **Unsteady only when walking** → gait/proprioceptive/cerebellar

### 16. Headache — *"Do you get headaches? Tell me about them."*

**Why we ask:** Overwhelmingly benign (tension, migraine) but hosts several neurological emergencies — the whole skill is pattern-matching the dangerous ones.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"Was it at its worst in the very first second — like being hit on the head?"* — Thunderclap onset → subarachnoid haemorrhage. It's the speed to maximum that flags it, not the severity
- **!** *"Any fever, or does it hurt to bend your neck forward?"* — Fever + neck stiffness + photophobia → meningitis
- **!** *"Is it worse first thing in the morning, or when you cough or bend forward? Any vomiting?"* — Worse on waking or with Valsalva → raised intracranial pressure
- **!** *"Is your scalp tender brushing your hair? Does your jaw ache when you chew?"* — Giant cell arteritis — ask this of anyone over 50 with a new headache
- **!** *"Any weakness, numbness, or trouble with speech or vision alongside it?"* — Any focal deficit → structural or vascular cause, not a primary headache
- *"How long does each one last, and how often do you get them?"* — Seconds → neuralgia · 15–180 min in bouts → cluster · 4–72 h → migraine · daily and constant → tension-type or medication-overuse
- *"Any warning before it starts — flashing lights or zigzag lines? Does light bother you?"* — Aura and photophobia → migraine
- *"How often are you taking painkillers for it?"* — Most days → medication-overuse headache — common, missed, reversible

> Full work-up → **headache** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Thunderclap — instant, worst-ever, "hit on the head"** → subarachnoid haemorrhage until excluded
- **Headache + fever + neck stiffness + photophobia ± rash** → meningitis
- **Worse in the morning / on coughing/bending, with vomiting** → raised intracranial pressure (mass)
- **New headache >50 with scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual loss** → giant cell arteritis
- **Unilateral, throbbing, with aura/nausea/photophobia, recurrent** → migraine
- **Bouts over weeks, strictly unilateral around one eye, with watering/red eye, nose running, restless and pacing** → cluster headache (the "alarm clock" headache — wakes them at the same time each night)
- **Band-like, bilateral, stress-related** → tension-type

**Red flag:** SNOOP: Systemic (fever, weight loss), Neuro deficit, Onset sudden, Older (>50 new), Papilloedema/positional/pattern change.

## Discriminator table: peripheral vs central vertigo

| Clue | Peripheral (vestibular) | Central (brainstem/cerebellar) |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing loss / tinnitus | Common | Usually absent |
| Other neuro signs | Absent | Present (diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, weakness) |
| Nystagmus | Horizontal, fatigable, suppressed by fixation | Vertical / direction-changing, not suppressed |
| Gait | Unsteady but can walk | Often cannot stand or walk |
| Examples | BPPV, vestibular neuritis, Ménière’s | Stroke / TIA — red flag |

## Quick-recall list (drill version)

BRAIN: confusion/delirium, memory, speech · CRANIAL: vision, hearing, smell, facial weakness, diplopia · CORD/NERVE: weakness (sudden vs gradual), numbness/tingling (distribution), coordination/balance/gait, falls · EPISODES: blackouts, seizures, dizziness (vertigo vs faint) · HEAD: headache (SOCRATES + SNOOP red flags)
