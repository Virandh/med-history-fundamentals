# Cardiovascular Systems Review

## The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

Think of the heart's jobs failing one at a time, moving outward from the chest:

> **PAIN → PUMP → RHYTHM → VESSELS → RISK**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | Ischaemic myocardium (or its mimics) | Chest pain |
| **Pump** | Forward failure + backward congestion | Dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND, ankle swelling, fatigue |
| **Rhythm** | Electrical system | Palpitations, syncope/presyncope |
| **Vessels** | Peripheral circulation | Claudication, cold/painful legs |
| **Risk** | The substrate for all of the above | HTN, diabetes, lipids, smoking, family history, rheumatic fever |

If you can say "pain, pump, rhythm, vessels, risk" you can rebuild all ~10 questions from first principles. The pump chunk has an internal logic too: congestion behind the left heart shows up in the lungs (dyspnoea → orthopnoea → PND, in order of severity), congestion behind the right heart shows up in the legs (oedema).

> **See also** — Respiratory — the cardiac-vs-respiratory dyspnoea split is shared between the two

## The questions

### 1. Chest pain — *"Have you had any pain, tightness or discomfort in your chest?"*

**Why we ask:** Chest pain is the cardinal symptom of myocardial ischaemia, but the character is what discriminates between the life-threats (ACS, dissection, PE, tension pneumothorax) and the benign mimics (MSK, GORD, anxiety). Always characterise fully with SOCRATES (Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associated symptoms, Timing, Exacerbating/relieving factors, Severity).

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Central, crushing/tight, radiating to jaw/left arm, on exertion, relieved by rest** → rules in angina (note: GTN relief alone is not specific — it also relieves oesophageal spasm and carries a placebo effect); if at rest >30 min with sweating/nausea → ACS
- **Tearing pain radiating to the back, abrupt maximal onset** → aortic dissection until proven otherwise
- **Sharp, worse on inspiration** → shifts you towards pleuritic causes (PE, pericarditis, pneumothorax) — note pericarditis pain classically eases sitting forward
- **Worse on palpation or specific movement** → MSK, but never rule out ACS on this alone
- **Burning, related to meals/lying flat, relieved by antacids** → GORD

**Red flag:** rest pain, crescendo pattern (unstable angina), associated syncope or diaphoresis, tearing interscapular pain.

### 2. Dyspnoea + exercise tolerance — *"Do you get short of breath? How far can you walk on the flat before stopping?"*

**Why we ask:** Exertional dyspnoea is the earliest symptom of left ventricular failure — pulmonary congestion raises the work of breathing before any signs appear. Quantifying exercise tolerance (metres on the flat, flights of stairs) gives you a severity yardstick (NYHA class) and a baseline to track change: "what could you do a year ago vs now?"

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Gradual decline in exercise tolerance + orthopnoea/PND/oedema** → rules in cardiac failure
- **Dyspnoea with wheeze, cough, sputum, smoking history** → shifts towards respiratory (COPD/asthma) — this is the great cardio-resp discriminator zone; see the table at the end
- **Sudden-onset dyspnoea** → think PE, pneumothorax, flash pulmonary oedema, arrhythmia
- **Dyspnoea on exertion with exertional chest tightness** → may be an angina equivalent, especially in diabetics and the elderly

**Red flag:** dyspnoea at rest, rapidly progressive over days.

---

### 3. Orthopnoea — *"How many pillows do you sleep with? What happens if you lie flat?"*

**Why we ask:** Lying flat returns pooled venous blood from the legs to the chest. A failing left ventricle can't handle the extra preload, so pulmonary congestion worsens within minutes of lying down. The pillow count is a semi-quantitative severity score.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Breathlessness within minutes of lying flat, relieved by sitting up** → strongly favours LVF over lung disease as the cause of dyspnoea
- COPD patients may also prefer sitting up, but their limitation is all-day and effort-related rather than positional — ask "is it lying flat itself that makes you breathless?"
- **New increase in pillow count** → decompensation

---

### 4. Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea — *"Do you ever wake at night gasping for air and need to sit up or go to the window?"*

**Why we ask:** During sleep, gradual reabsorption of interstitial fluid and nocturnal reduction in adrenergic drive let pulmonary oedema accumulate; the patient wakes 1–2 hours in, suffocating, and must sit or stand for several minutes to recover. It is one of the more specific congestion symptoms for left heart failure (Talley quantifies only orthopnoea, which strongly favours LVF over lung disease).

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Classic story (waking gasping, relieved slowly by sitting/standing)** → rules in LVF with high specificity
- Distinguish from nocturnal asthma (wheeze-predominant, often with cough, responds to inhaler) and OSA (witnessed apnoeas, snoring, unrefreshed sleep — the patient's partner often gives the history)

**Red flag:** new PND = decompensated heart failure until proven otherwise.

---

### 5. Ankle swelling / oedema — *"Any swelling of the ankles or legs? Worse through the day?"*

**Why we ask:** Right heart congestion raises venous pressure, pushing fluid into dependent tissue. Gravity makes it worst in the evening and after standing.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Bilateral, pitting, worse by evening, with other failure symptoms** → rules in congestive cardiac failure
- **Unilateral swelling** → think DVT or local cause (cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst) — a completely different pathway
- **Bilateral oedema without dyspnoea** → consider hypoalbuminaemia (nephrotic, liver disease), calcium-channel blockers (amlodipine is a classic), venous insufficiency, or pelvic obstruction
- Ask about abdominal swelling and weight gain — ascites and rapid weight gain suggest significant fluid retention

---

### 6. Palpitations — *"Are you ever aware of your heart racing, thumping or skipping? Can you tap out the rhythm?"*

**Why we ask:** "Palpitations" means ten different things to ten patients. The tapped rhythm, mode of onset/offset, and company the symptom keeps let you separate benign ectopics from dangerous arrhythmias.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Fast, irregularly irregular** → AF — ask about precipitants (alcohol, caffeine, thyroid symptoms) and stroke risk factors
- **Abrupt on/off, regular, fast; may terminate with vagal manoeuvres** → SVT
- **Occasional "missed beat then a thump" at rest, disappearing on exercise** → benign ectopics
- **Gradual onset with anxiety, hyperventilation, tingling** → panic attack — but exclude arrhythmia first, not the other way round
- **Palpitations with syncope or chest pain** → possible VT / structural disease

**Red flag:** palpitations + syncope, family history of sudden death (channelopathies, HOCM), known structural heart disease.

---

### 7. Syncope / presyncope — *"Have you ever blacked out or felt you were about to? What were you doing?"*

**Why we ask:** Cardiac syncope (arrhythmia, outflow obstruction) kills; vasovagal syncope doesn't. The circumstances are almost the whole diagnosis.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Syncope during exertion** → rules in sinister causes: aortic stenosis, HOCM, VT — never dismiss it
- **No warning, rapid full recovery** → arrhythmic (Stokes–Adams); note injury is uncommon in cardiac syncope, so its absence does not reassure
- **Prodrome of nausea, sweating, greying vision, triggered by standing/pain/emotion, rapid recovery** → vasovagal — rules out cardiac cause with reasonable confidence
- **On standing** → orthostatic hypotension — review antihypertensives, diuretics, and ask about fluid losses
- **Lateral tongue-biting and post-ictal confusion/drowsiness** → shifts to seizure, a different system entirely (jerk duration is not a reliable discriminator: brief clonic jerks occur in ordinary convulsive syncope, and prolonged jerking actually suggests non-epileptic seizures)

**Red flag:** exertional syncope, syncope with no prodrome, family history of sudden cardiac death.

---

### 8. Intermittent claudication — *"Do you get cramping calf or buttock pain on walking that eases with rest?"*

**Why we ask:** The peripheral arteries share the same atherosclerotic disease as the coronaries — claudication marks the patient as a vasculopath (their biggest killer is MI, not the leg). Claudication distance is the severity yardstick.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Reproducible cramping at a fixed walking distance, relieved within minutes of standing still** → rules in peripheral arterial disease
- **Pain relieved only by sitting/leaning forward (not just stopping) and associated with back pain** → spinal claudication (canal stenosis)
- **Rest pain, worse at night, relieved by hanging the foot out of bed** → critical limb ischaemia — red flag, needs urgent vascular review
- Ask also about cold feet, colour change, ulcers that won't heal

**Red flag:** Rest pain / critical limb ischaemia needs urgent vascular review.

---

### 9. Fatigue — *"How is your energy? More tired than you used to be?"*

**Why we ask:** Low cardiac output presents as fatigue before frank dyspnoea in some patients (especially the elderly). Non-specific alone — its value is in aggregate with the pump-failure cluster. Also a common symptom of beta-blocker therapy — review the medication list.

---

### 10. Risk factor screen — *"High blood pressure, diabetes or cholesterol? Do you smoke? Heart disease in the family? Rheumatic fever as a child?"*

**Why we ask:** Risk factors set the pre-test probability for everything above. Chest pain in a 55-year-old diabetic smoker with a father who died at 50 is a different question to the same pain in a 25-year-old with none of these.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- Modifiable: hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, smoking (pack-years), obesity, physical inactivity
- Non-modifiable: age, male sex, family history of premature IHD (first-degree relative, male <55 / female <65)
- **Rheumatic fever in childhood** → valvular disease decades later (still relevant in older patients and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities)

## Discriminator table: is the dyspnoea cardiac or respiratory?

| Clue | Points to cardiac | Points to respiratory |
|---|---|---|
| Orthopnoea / PND | Strongly cardiac | Uncommon (COPD sits up for effort, not position) |
| Ankle swelling | Yes (with the above) | Only in cor pulmonale — late |
| Wheeze | "Cardiac wheeze" possible in pulmonary oedema | Typical of asthma/COPD |
| Cough | Dry, nocturnal, frothy ± pink sputum | Productive, chronic, purulent in infection |
| Chest pain | Exertional tightness (angina) | Pleuritic (sharp, breath-related) |
| History | IHD, HTN, AF, murmur | Smoking, atopy, occupational exposure |

## Quick-recall list (drill version)

PAIN: chest pain (SOCRATES) · PUMP: dyspnoea + exercise tolerance, orthopnoea, PND, ankle swelling, fatigue · RHYTHM: palpitations, syncope/presyncope · VESSELS: claudication, rest pain, cold feet · RISK: HTN, DM, lipids, smoking, FHx, rheumatic fever
