# Cardiovascular Systems Review

## The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

Think of the heart's jobs failing one at a time, moving outward from the chest:

> **PAIN → PUMP → RHYTHM → VESSELS → RISK**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | Ischaemic myocardium (or its mimics) | Chest pain |
| **Pump** | Forward failure + backward congestion | Dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND, ankle swelling, fatigue |
| **Rhythm** | Electrical system | Palpitations, syncope/presyncope |
| **Vessels** | Peripheral circulation | Claudication, cold/painful legs |
| **Risk** | The substrate for all of the above | HTN, diabetes, lipids, smoking, family history, rheumatic fever |

If you can say "pain, pump, rhythm, vessels, risk" you can rebuild all ~10 questions from first principles. The pump chunk has an internal logic too: congestion behind the left heart shows up in the lungs (dyspnoea → orthopnoea → PND, in order of severity), congestion behind the right heart shows up in the legs (oedema).

> **See also** — Respiratory — the cardiac-vs-respiratory dyspnoea split is shared between the two

## The questions

### 1. Chest pain — *"Have you had any pain, tightness or discomfort in your chest?"*

**Why we ask:** Chest pain is the cardinal symptom of myocardial ischaemia, but the character is what discriminates between the life-threats (ACS, dissection, PE, tension pneumothorax) and the benign mimics (MSK, GORD, anxiety). Always characterise fully with SOCRATES (Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associated symptoms, Timing, Exacerbating/relieving factors, Severity).

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Can you describe the pain — sharp or dull, heavy or tight?"* — Character does most of the work: tight/heavy/crushing → ischaemic pattern · sharp/stabbing → pleuritic or pericardial · burning → reflux
- *"When do you get it — out of the blue, or with exertion? Worse after a meal or in the cold?"* — Exertional, reproducible → angina · unrelated to exertion, worse lying down or after meals → reflux
- **!** *"How long does it last, and does it ease with rest?"* — Minutes, eases with rest → stable angina · over 30 min at rest, unrelieved → acute coronary syndrome
- **!** *"Does it radiate anywhere — jaw, arms, back?"* — Radiation to jaw/left arm → cardiac · sudden tearing pain radiating to the back → aortic dissection
- **!** *"Is it coming on with less effort than before, or now at rest?"* — A crescendo pattern or pain at rest → unstable angina/ACS, needs urgent assessment
- **!** *"Any sweating, nausea, or feeling faint with it?"* — Sweating, nausea and breathlessness alongside the pain raise the likelihood of ACS
- *"Is it affected by breathing, movement, or position?"* — Worse on inspiration → pleuritic (PE, pericarditis, pneumothorax); worse on movement/palpation → musculoskeletal — but never rule out ACS on this alone
- *"Have you taken anything for it — does a spray under the tongue, or an antacid, help?"* — GTN relief is not specific — it also eases oesophageal spasm and carries a placebo effect — so use it to support, not confirm, an ischaemic cause

> Full work-up → **chest-pain** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Central, crushing/tight, radiating to jaw/left arm, on exertion, relieved by rest** → rules in angina (note: GTN relief alone is not specific — it also relieves oesophageal spasm and carries a placebo effect); if at rest >30 min with sweating/nausea → ACS
- **Tearing pain radiating to the back, abrupt maximal onset** → aortic dissection until proven otherwise
- **Sharp, worse on inspiration** → shifts you towards pleuritic causes (PE, pericarditis, pneumothorax) — note pericarditis pain classically eases sitting forward
- **Worse on palpation or specific movement** → MSK, but never rule out ACS on this alone
- **Burning, related to meals/lying flat, relieved by antacids** → GORD

**Red flag:** rest pain, crescendo pattern (unstable angina), associated syncope or diaphoresis, tearing interscapular pain.

### 2. Dyspnoea + exercise tolerance — *"Do you get short of breath? How far can you walk on the flat before stopping?"*

**Why we ask:** Exertional dyspnoea is the earliest symptom of left ventricular failure — pulmonary congestion raises the work of breathing before any signs appear. Quantifying exercise tolerance (metres on the flat, flights of stairs) gives you a severity yardstick (NYHA class) and a baseline to track change: "what could you do a year ago vs now?"

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How far can you walk on the flat, or how many stairs, before you're stopped? Compared with a year ago?"* — Quantify — this maps onto NYHA class and gives a trajectory, more reliable than the patient's own severity grading
- *"Did it come on suddenly, or has it crept up gradually?"* — Sudden → MI, pulmonary oedema, arrhythmia, PE · gradual → progressive left ventricular impairment
- **!** *"Any tightness in the chest when you're breathless?"* — Dyspnoea with exertional chest tightness may be an angina equivalent, especially in diabetics and the elderly
- *"Do you get breathless lying flat, or wake at night gasping?"* — Orthopnoea/PND strongly favour a cardiac over a respiratory cause — pursue with those specific questions
- *"Any wheeze, cough, sputum, or a smoking history?"* — Pulls the picture toward a respiratory cause (COPD/asthma) rather than cardiac — the classic cardio-resp discriminator zone
- *"Any past heart attacks, angina, high blood pressure, or heavy alcohol use?"* — Known IHD points to reduced LV contractility; hypertension or heavy alcohol intake point to hypertensive heart disease or alcoholic cardiomyopathy
- *"Does it feel like you can't get a big enough breath in, coming on at rest rather than with exertion?"* — Suggests anxiety-related breathlessness — a diagnosis of exclusion, not the first assumption

> Full work-up → **breathlessness** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Gradual decline in exercise tolerance + orthopnoea/PND/oedema** → rules in cardiac failure
- **Dyspnoea with wheeze, cough, sputum, smoking history** → shifts towards respiratory (COPD/asthma) — this is the great cardio-resp discriminator zone; see the table at the end
- **Sudden-onset dyspnoea** → think PE, pneumothorax, flash pulmonary oedema, arrhythmia
- **Dyspnoea on exertion with exertional chest tightness** → may be an angina equivalent, especially in diabetics and the elderly

**Red flag:** dyspnoea at rest, rapidly progressive over days.

### 3. Orthopnoea — *"How many pillows do you sleep with? What happens if you lie flat?"*

**Why we ask:** Lying flat returns pooled venous blood from the legs to the chest. A failing left ventricle can't handle the extra preload, so pulmonary congestion worsens within minutes of lying down. The pillow count is a semi-quantitative severity score.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How many pillows do you use, and has that changed recently?"* — Pillow count is a semi-quantitative severity score — a new increase signals decompensation
- *"Is it lying flat itself that brings it on, or are you just generally limited by effort?"* — True orthopnoea is positional and rapid (minutes) — COPD patients may also prefer sitting up, but their limitation is effort-related and all-day, not positional
- *"How quickly does it come on after lying down, and does sitting up relieve it?"* — Onset within minutes of lying flat, relieved by sitting up, strongly favours LVF over lung disease as the cause of dyspnoea

> Full work-up → **breathlessness** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Breathlessness within minutes of lying flat, relieved by sitting up** → strongly favours LVF over lung disease as the cause of dyspnoea
- COPD patients may also prefer sitting up, but their limitation is all-day and effort-related rather than positional — ask "is it lying flat itself that makes you breathless?"
- **New increase in pillow count** → decompensation

### 4. Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea — *"Do you ever wake at night gasping for air and need to sit up or go to the window?"*

**Why we ask:** During sleep, gradual reabsorption of interstitial fluid and nocturnal reduction in adrenergic drive let pulmonary oedema accumulate; the patient wakes 1–2 hours in, suffocating, and must sit or stand for several minutes to recover. It is one of the more specific congestion symptoms for left heart failure (Talley quantifies only orthopnoea, which strongly favours LVF over lung disease).

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How long after falling asleep does it happen, and what do you have to do to recover — sit up, stand, go to the window?"* — Waking 1–2 hours in, gasping, needing several minutes upright to recover, is the classic pattern — highly specific for LVF
- *"Is it wheeze that wakes you, and does an inhaler help?"* — Wheeze-predominant, with cough, responsive to an inhaler, points to nocturnal asthma instead
- *"Does your partner notice loud snoring or pauses in your breathing?"* — Witnessed apnoeas/snoring with unrefreshed sleep points to OSA rather than PND
- **!** *"Is this new, or has it happened before?"* — New PND = decompensated heart failure until proven otherwise

> Full work-up → **breathlessness** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Classic story (waking gasping, relieved slowly by sitting/standing)** → rules in LVF with high specificity
- Distinguish from nocturnal asthma (wheeze-predominant, often with cough, responds to inhaler) and OSA (witnessed apnoeas, snoring, unrefreshed sleep — the patient's partner often gives the history)

**Red flag:** new PND = decompensated heart failure until proven otherwise.

### 5. Ankle swelling / oedema — *"Any swelling of the ankles or legs? Worse through the day?"*

**Why we ask:** Right heart congestion raises venous pressure, pushing fluid into dependent tissue. Gravity makes it worst in the evening and after standing.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"Is it both ankles, or just one?"* — Bilateral → systemic/cardiac cause · unilateral → DVT or a local cause (cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst) — a completely different pathway
- *"Worse by evening, better after a night lying flat?"* — The classic pattern of dependent, cardiac-origin oedema
- *"Any breathlessness, orthopnoea or PND alongside it?"* — Oedema plus the pump-failure cluster rules in congestive cardiac failure — oedema alone is poorly correlated with heart failure
- *"Any recent weight gain, or swelling of the abdomen?"* — Rapid weight gain (3 kg or more) and ascites suggest significant fluid retention
- *"Any new medications, especially for blood pressure?"* — Calcium-channel blockers (e.g. amlodipine) are a classic drug cause of peripheral oedema unrelated to the heart

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Bilateral, pitting, worse by evening, with other failure symptoms** → rules in congestive cardiac failure
- **Unilateral swelling** → think DVT or local cause (cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst) — a completely different pathway
- **Bilateral oedema without dyspnoea** → consider hypoalbuminaemia (nephrotic, liver disease), calcium-channel blockers (amlodipine is a classic), venous insufficiency, or pelvic obstruction
- Ask about abdominal swelling and weight gain — ascites and rapid weight gain suggest significant fluid retention

### 6. Fatigue — *"How is your energy? More tired than you used to be?"*

**Why we ask:** Low cardiac output presents as fatigue before frank dyspnoea in some patients (especially the elderly). Non-specific alone — its value is in aggregate with the pump-failure cluster. Also a common symptom of beta-blocker therapy — review the medication list.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is this fatigue different from feeling normal tiredness — is it limiting what you can do?"* — Low-output fatigue tracks with exertion and may precede frank dyspnoea, especially in the elderly
- *"Any breathlessness, ankle swelling, or chest symptoms alongside it?"* — Fatigue is non-specific alone — its value is in aggregate with the pump-failure cluster
- *"Are you on a beta-blocker, or any new medication?"* — Beta-blockers commonly cause fatigue — review the medication list before assuming cardiac decompensation
- *"Any other explanation — poor sleep, low mood, or heavy periods/bleeding?"* — Rules out the other common causes of fatigue — anaemia, depression, poor sleep — before anchoring on cardiac output

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Fatigue accompanying dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND or oedema** → part of the low-output/congestive picture, and may precede frank breathlessness, especially in the elderly
- **Fatigue in isolation, or explained by poor sleep, anaemia, depression or a new beta-blocker** → look outside cardiac failure first

### 7. Palpitations — *"Are you ever aware of your heart racing, thumping or skipping? Can you tap out the rhythm?"*

**Why we ask:** "Palpitations" means ten different things to ten patients. The tapped rhythm, mode of onset/offset, and company the symptom keeps let you separate benign ectopics from dangerous arrhythmias.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Can you tap out the rhythm for me — is it fast or slow, regular or irregular?"* — The tapped rhythm is often more useful than the verbal description — irregularly irregular → AF · very fast and regular → SVT/VT
- *"How does it start and stop — suddenly, or does it build up gradually?"* — Abrupt on/off → arrhythmia (SVT/VT) · gradual → sinus tachycardia (anxiety, exertion, fever)
- *"How long do episodes last, and how often do they happen?"* — Brief and occasional suggests resting ectopics; sustained episodes need more urgent characterisation
- **!** *"Any chest tightness or breathlessness with it?"* — Palpitations + chest tightness/dyspnoea raise concern for a haemodynamically significant arrhythmia
- **!** *"Have you ever blacked out, or felt about to, during an episode?"* — Palpitations + syncope suggest VT, especially with known structural heart disease
- *"Any triggers — caffeine, alcohol, stress — or things that stop it, like holding your breath or coughing?"* — Response to vagal manoeuvres (Valsalva, cold water, coughing) supports SVT
- *"Has a smartwatch or home monitor recorded anything?"* — Increasingly common corroborating evidence, but these devices are often confused by ectopic beats — don't over-interpret an unreviewed alert
- **!** *"Any family history of unexplained sudden death, or known heart disease yourself?"* — Raises concern for inherited channelopathies (long QT, Brugada) or structural disease underlying the arrhythmia

> Full work-up → **palpitations** approach

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Fast, irregularly irregular** → AF — ask about precipitants (alcohol, caffeine, thyroid symptoms) and stroke risk factors
- **Abrupt on/off, regular, fast; may terminate with vagal manoeuvres** → SVT
- **Occasional "missed beat then a thump" at rest, disappearing on exercise** → benign ectopics
- **Gradual onset with anxiety, hyperventilation, tingling** → panic attack — but exclude arrhythmia first, not the other way round
- **Palpitations with syncope or chest pain** → possible VT / structural disease

**Red flag:** palpitations + syncope, family history of sudden death (channelopathies, HOCM), known structural heart disease.

### 8. Syncope / presyncope — *"Have you ever blacked out or felt you were about to? What were you doing?"*

**Why we ask:** Cardiac syncope (arrhythmia, outflow obstruction) kills; vasovagal syncope doesn't. The circumstances are almost the whole diagnosis.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"What were you doing right when it happened?"* — Circumstances are almost the whole diagnosis — exertional syncope is sinister (AS, HOCM, VT); standing, coughing or micturition point to reflex/situational causes
- *"Was there any warning — nausea, sweating, tunnel vision — or did it come out of nowhere?"* — A vasovagal prodrome rules out a cardiac cause with reasonable confidence; no warning at all suggests an arrhythmic (Stokes–Adams) cause
- *"How quickly did you recover, and were you confused or drowsy afterwards?"* — Rapid, full recovery favours cardiac/vasovagal; slow recovery with confusion favours seizure or a neurological cause
- *"Did you bite your tongue, or did anyone see jerking movements?"* — Lateral tongue-biting and prolonged jerking suggest seizure — though brief clonic jerks can occur in ordinary convulsive syncope, so don't over-weight jerking alone
- **!** *"Any chest pain, palpitations, or known heart disease beforehand?"* — Suggests an arrhythmic or ischaemic cause, especially with a background of structural heart disease
- *"Did it happen on standing up, or after a meal or dehydration? Any recent change to your blood pressure tablets?"* — Points to orthostatic hypotension — review antihypertensives, diuretics and fluid losses
- **!** *"Any family history of sudden unexplained death at a young age?"* — Raises concern for inherited channelopathies (long/short QT, Brugada syndrome)
- *"Has anyone filmed an episode, or do you wear a device that might have recorded it?"* — A bystander video or smartwatch ECG can be the single most useful piece of evidence you'll get

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Syncope during exertion** → rules in sinister causes: aortic stenosis, HOCM, VT — never dismiss it
- **No warning, rapid full recovery** → arrhythmic (Stokes–Adams); note injury is uncommon in cardiac syncope, so its absence does not reassure
- **Prodrome of nausea, sweating, greying vision, triggered by standing/pain/emotion, rapid recovery** → vasovagal — rules out cardiac cause with reasonable confidence
- **On standing** → orthostatic hypotension — review antihypertensives, diuretics, and ask about fluid losses
- **Lateral tongue-biting and post-ictal confusion/drowsiness** → shifts to seizure, a different system entirely (jerk duration is not a reliable discriminator: brief clonic jerks occur in ordinary convulsive syncope, and prolonged jerking actually suggests non-epileptic seizures)

**Red flag:** exertional syncope, syncope with no prodrome, family history of sudden cardiac death.

### 9. Intermittent claudication — *"Do you get cramping calf or buttock pain on walking that eases with rest?"*

**Why we ask:** The peripheral arteries share the same atherosclerotic disease as the coronaries — claudication marks the patient as a vasculopath (their biggest killer is MI, not the leg). Claudication distance is the severity yardstick.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Where do you feel it — calf, thigh, or buttock — and how far can you walk before it starts?"* — Location clues the level of arterial disease; claudication distance is the severity yardstick
- *"Does it ease within a few minutes of stopping, or do you need to sit down first?"* — Relief on simply standing still → vascular claudication · relief only on sitting/leaning forward, with back pain → spinal (pseudo-)claudication from canal stenosis
- **!** *"Does the pain ever happen at rest, especially at night, and does hanging your foot out of bed help?"* — Rest pain relieved by dependency → critical limb ischaemia, needs urgent vascular review
- *"Any colour change, coldness, or sores/ulcers on your feet that won't heal?"* — Pallor, coldness and non-healing ulcers complete the peripheral arterial disease picture (the six Ps)
- *"Do you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a smoking history?"* — The same atherosclerotic risk factors — this patient's biggest killer is MI, not the leg

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Reproducible cramping at a fixed walking distance, relieved within minutes of standing still** → rules in peripheral arterial disease
- **Pain relieved only by sitting/leaning forward (not just stopping) and associated with back pain** → spinal claudication (canal stenosis)
- **Rest pain, worse at night, relieved by hanging the foot out of bed** → critical limb ischaemia — red flag, needs urgent vascular review
- Ask also about cold feet, colour change, ulcers that won't heal

**Red flag:** Rest pain / critical limb ischaemia needs urgent vascular review.

---

### 10. Modifiable risk factors — *"High blood pressure, diabetes or cholesterol? Do you smoke? How's your weight and activity level?"*

**Why we ask:** These set the pre-test probability for everything above and are the targets of secondary prevention.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, smoking (quantify pack-years), obesity, physical inactivity** → each independently raises atherosclerotic risk

---

### 11. Family history of premature IHD — *"Any heart disease in the family — particularly at a young age?"*

**Why we ask:** A first-degree relative with premature IHD (male <55, female <65) raises the patient's own risk independent of their other risk factors.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **First-degree relative, male <55 / female <65, with IHD** → premature family history, a non-modifiable risk factor

---

### 12. Rheumatic fever — *"Did you have rheumatic fever as a child?"*

**Why we ask:** Rheumatic fever causes valvular disease that only declares itself decades later — still relevant in older patients and in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where rates remain high.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Rheumatic fever in childhood** → valvular disease (esp. mitral) decades later

## Discriminator table: is the dyspnoea cardiac or respiratory?

| Clue | Points to cardiac | Points to respiratory |
|---|---|---|
| Orthopnoea / PND | Strongly cardiac | Uncommon (COPD sits up for effort, not position) |
| Ankle swelling | Yes (with the above) | Only in cor pulmonale — late |
| Wheeze | "Cardiac wheeze" possible in pulmonary oedema | Typical of asthma/COPD |
| Cough | Dry, nocturnal, frothy ± pink sputum | Productive, chronic, purulent in infection |
| Chest pain | Exertional tightness (angina) | Pleuritic (sharp, breath-related) |
| History | IHD, HTN, AF, murmur | Smoking, atopy, occupational exposure |

## Quick-recall list (drill version)

PAIN: chest pain (SOCRATES) · PUMP: dyspnoea + exercise tolerance, orthopnoea, PND, ankle swelling, fatigue · RHYTHM: palpitations, syncope/presyncope · VESSELS: claudication, rest pain, cold feet · RISK: HTN, DM, lipids, smoking, FHx, rheumatic fever
