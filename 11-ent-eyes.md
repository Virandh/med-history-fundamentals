# ENT & Eyes Systems Review

## The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

Two regional screens that often sit at the end of the systems review. Take them organ by organ:

> **EARS → NOSE → THROAT → EYES**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Ears** | Hearing/balance | Hearing loss, tinnitus, discharge, earache, vertigo |
| **Nose** | Nasal/sinus | Blockage, discharge, epistaxis, smell, facial pain |
| **Throat** | Pharynx/larynx | Sore throat, hoarseness, swallowing, neck lumps |
| **Eyes** | Vision | Visual loss, pain, red eye, double vision, discharge |

The red-flag threads: **sudden loss** (sudden hearing loss, sudden visual loss = emergencies), **unilateral persistent symptoms** (unilateral hearing loss/nasal obstruction/neck lump → tumour), and **painful red eye** (sight-threatening).

---

## The questions

### 1. Hearing loss — *"Has your hearing changed — one ear or both? Sudden or gradual?"*

**Why we ask:** The tempo and laterality triage it — from wax to a treatable emergency to a tumour.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"One ear or both, and did it come on suddenly or gradually?"* — Sudden → possible emergency, needs same-day ENT; gradual, bilateral, high-frequency in an older person → presbyacusis
- *"How long has this been a problem — days, or years?"* — Distinguishes an acute event from a longstanding/congenital problem
- *"Any exposure to loud noise at work or in hobbies, past or present?"* — Chronic noise exposure is a common, often under-recognised, cause of sensorineural loss
- *"Any ringing in the ears, or dizziness with it?"* — Tinnitus + hearing loss + vertigo → Ménière's disease
- *"Any ear pain, discharge, or a recent cold?"* — Suggests a conductive cause — wax, effusion, infection, perforation — rather than sensorineural
- *"Any family history of deafness, or problems with wax in the past?"* — Family history raises otosclerosis; past wax problems point to a recurring conductive cause
- *"Have you been on any medications that can affect hearing — antibiotics, chemotherapy, aspirin, loop diuretics?"* — Ototoxic drugs are a common, reversible-if-caught-early cause
- *"Any head injury, or a serious infection like meningitis?"* — Trauma or past meningitis can directly damage the cochlea or acoustic nerve

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Gradual, bilateral, high-frequency, older/noise exposure** → presbyacusis/noise-induced (sensorineural)
- **Sudden sensorineural loss** → emergency — needs same-day ENT (treatable window)
- **Unilateral persistent sensorineural loss ± tinnitus** → exclude acoustic neuroma
- **Conductive loss with blockage/discharge** → wax, effusion, perforation, otosclerosis

**Red flag:** Sudden sensorineural hearing loss has a treatable window — refer same day.

### 2. Tinnitus — *"Any ringing or buzzing in the ears? One side or both — constant, or pulsing with your heartbeat?"*

**Why we ask:** Pulsatile or strictly unilateral tinnitus needs vascular/tumour work-up; most tinnitus tracks with hearing loss or an ototoxic drug (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, aspirin/NSAIDs, quinine, some chemotherapy agents).

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it constant, or does it pulse in time with your heartbeat?"* — Pulsatile tinnitus needs a vascular work-up, unlike the common non-pulsatile kind
- *"One side or both, and is your hearing affected too?"* — Strictly unilateral, or with hearing loss/vertigo, needs investigation — Ménière's, or rarely an acoustic neuroma
- *"Any medications that could cause this — aspirin, some antibiotics, or diuretics?"* — A long list of ototoxic drugs cause tinnitus, and it often resolves on stopping them

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Pulsatile or strictly unilateral** → needs investigation (vascular anomaly, acoustic neuroma)
- **Tinnitus + hearing loss + vertigo** → Ménière's
- **Bilateral, non-pulsatile, with noise exposure or an ototoxic drug on the list** → benign/drug-related

---

### 3. Ear discharge & earache — *"Any discharge or pain from the ear?"*

**Why we ask:** Localises within the outer, middle or inner ear and separates simple infection from the sinister chronic discharge of cholesteatoma.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is there pain, and any fever with the discharge?"* — Discharge + pain + fever → otitis externa or media, usually straightforward infection
- *"Has this been going on for a long time, and does it smell foul?"* — Chronic, foul-smelling discharge with hearing loss → cholesteatoma, needs ENT referral rather than just antibiotics
- *"Do you wear hearing aids, or use cotton buds or other objects in your ear?"* — Common triggers for chronic otitis externa through local irritation

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Discharge + pain + fever** → otitis externa/media
- **Foul, chronic discharge + hearing loss** → cholesteatoma — needs ENT referral

---

### 4. Vertigo — *"Do you get spells where the room feels like it's spinning?"*

**Why we ask:** True rotational vertigo localises to the vestibular system or its central connections — cross-reference the Neurological Dizziness/vertigo entry for the full vertigo-vs-presyncope work-up.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Does it come with a specific head movement, and how long does each spell last?"* — Brief, positional spells → BPPV
- *"Any hearing loss or ringing in the ears with it?"* — Present → Ménière's or labyrinthitis; absent → vestibular neuritis or BPPV more likely
- **!** *"Any weakness, double vision, or slurred speech alongside it?"* — Any of these points to a central cause rather than the inner ear — a red flag

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Vertigo + hearing loss + tinnitus** → Ménière's
- **Brief, positional, vertigo alone** → BPPV

---

### 5. Nasal blockage & discharge — *"Is your nose blocked or running? One side or both?"*

**Why we ask:** Bilateral symptoms are usually allergic or infective; persistent unilateral symptoms need a tumour excluded.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"One side, or both?"* — Bilateral → usually allergic/infective; unilateral and persistent → exclude a nasal/sinus tumour
- *"Is it worse at certain times of year, and does it come with sneezing or itchy eyes?"* — Seasonal pattern with sneezing/itch → allergic rhinitis
- *"Any past nasal injury, or could something be stuck in there?"* — A structural cause (deviated septum) or foreign body, especially relevant in children

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Bilateral blockage + sneezing + itch + clear discharge, seasonal** → allergic rhinitis
- **Unilateral blockage or discharge, persistent** → exclude nasal/sinus tumour
- **Foreign body (children) or deviated septum** → structural, usually unilateral and longstanding

**Red flag:** Persistent unilateral nasal obstruction or discharge → tumour until excluded.

---

### 6. Epistaxis — *"Have you ever had nosebleeds? How often, how much, and do they stop easily?"*

**Why we ask:** Most epistaxis is local (trauma, nose-picking, dry mucosa), but recurrent, severe or bilateral bleeding raises anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, bleeding disorders and, rarely, a nasal tumour.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Recurrent, minor, with dry air/nose-picking** → local trauma, the commonest cause
- **On antiplatelets/anticoagulants, or bruising elsewhere** → medication effect or a bleeding disorder (haematological malignancy, haemophilia)
- **Severe enough to need transfusion, or unilateral and persistent in an older adult** → exclude nasal tumour
- Hypertension is commonly cited as a cause but the causal link is contested — treat it as a soft association, not an established one

---

### 7. Loss of smell — *"Any change in your sense of smell — or taste, since the two often go together?"*

**Why we ask:** Anosmia localises to mechanical obstruction (polyps), infection (including post-viral/COVID), or trauma if complete; isolated anosmia in an older adult can be an early non-motor feature of neurodegenerative disease.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Did this come on suddenly after a cold, or has it developed slowly over time?"* — Post-viral (including post-COVID) loss usually recovers over weeks to months; slow, isolated loss in an older adult with no nasal symptoms raises a neurodegenerative cause
- *"Any nasal blockage or known polyps?"* — Local/mechanical obstruction is the commonest treatable cause

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **With nasal obstruction or known polyps** → local/mechanical
- **Post-viral (including post-COVID)** → common, usually recovers over weeks to months
- **Isolated, no nasal symptoms, older adult** → consider a neurodegenerative cause

---

### 8. Facial pain / pressure — *"Any pain or pressure over your face or sinuses — worse bending forward?"*

**Why we ask:** Localises inflammation to the paranasal sinuses; pain above the eye points to frontal sinusitis, periorbital pain to ethmoid sinusitis.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it worse bending forward, and any purulent discharge or maxillary toothache?"* — The classic sinusitis triad — bending-forward pain, purulent discharge, maxillary toothache
- **!** *"Any periorbital swelling, visual change, or severe headache with it?"* — Suggests orbital or intracranial spread of sinusitis — an emergency, not simple sinusitis
- *"Any itchy eyes or sneezing, without much discharge?"* — Favours allergic rhinitis over true sinusitis

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Facial pain/pressure + purulent nasal discharge + maxillary toothache, worse bending forward, post-URTI** → sinusitis
- **Itchy eyes + sneezing, no purulent discharge** → favours allergic rhinitis over sinusitis

**Red flag:** Periorbital swelling, visual change or severe headache with sinusitis → orbital or intracranial spread — emergency.

---

### 9. Sore throat — *"Any sore throat? Fever, or trouble opening your mouth?"*

**Why we ask:** Most sore throat is viral pharyngitis; the job is to catch the bacterial minority and the rare deep-space emergency.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Any fever, or white patches/pus on your tonsils?"* — Fever + tonsillar exudate → bacterial pharyngitis/tonsillitis, or in adolescents, glandular fever (EBV)
- **!** *"Is it one-sided and severe, with trouble opening your mouth or drooling?"* — Unilateral severe pain + trismus + drooling → quinsy (peritonsillar abscess)
- **!** *"Any difficulty breathing, or a harsh noise when you breathe in?"* — Stridor with a sore throat → epiglottitis, a red flag — do not examine the throat if suspected

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Acute sore throat + fever + tonsillar exudate** → bacterial pharyngitis/tonsillitis (group A strep), or in adolescents, EBV
- **Unilateral severe pain + trismus + drooling** → quinsy (peritonsillar abscess) — red flag
- **Sore throat + odynophagia + fever ± stridor** → epiglottitis — red flag, do not examine the throat

**Red flag:** Trismus/drooling (quinsy) or stridor with sore throat (epiglottitis) are emergencies.

---

### 10. Dysphagia / odynophagia — *"Any difficulty or pain swallowing?"*

**Why we ask:** Progressive dysphagia with weight loss is oesophageal/pharyngeal cancer until excluded — cross-reference the GI dysphagia entry for the solid-vs-liquid localisation.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it solids only, or solids and liquids together?"* — Solids only → mechanical (stricture, tumour); solids and liquids from the start → a motor problem
- **!** *"Has it been getting steadily worse, and any weight loss?"* — Progressive dysphagia + weight loss → oesophageal/pharyngeal malignancy until excluded
- *"Is it painful to swallow, and does it come with a sore throat or fever?"* — Odynophagia with a sore throat and fever points to pharyngitis or tonsillitis rather than a structural cause

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Progressive dysphagia + weight loss** → oesophageal/pharyngeal malignancy
- **Painful swallowing (odynophagia)** → pharyngitis, tonsillitis, or rarely epiglottitis

---

### 11. Hoarseness — *"Has your voice changed or become hoarse?"*

**Why we ask:** Persistent hoarseness >3 weeks needs laryngoscopy — in a smoker it can be the first sign of laryngeal or lung cancer via recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement (cross-reference the Respiratory hoarseness entry).

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"How long has this lasted, and is it getting worse?"* — Short history → laryngitis; persistent beyond 3 weeks needs laryngoscopy
- *"Do you smoke, and any recent anaesthetic or intubation, or heavy voice use?"* — Smoking raises laryngeal/lung cancer; recent intubation raises injury; heavy voice use raises overuse or vocal cord nodules
- *"Any thyroid symptoms, or a steroid inhaler you use?"* — Hypothyroidism and inhaled corticosteroid use are both reversible causes worth checking before further work-up

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Persistent hoarseness >3 weeks in a smoker** → laryngeal or lung cancer until excluded
- **Hoarseness after a URTI, resolving within days–weeks** → laryngitis — benign

**Red flag:** Persistent hoarseness >3 weeks needs laryngoscopy.

---

### 12. Neck lump — *"Any lumps or swelling in the neck?"*

**Why we ask:** A persistent unilateral neck lump in an adult is malignancy until excluded — nodal, thyroid or salivary.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How long has it been there, and has it changed in size?"* — Persistent and enlarging over weeks is more concerning than a lump present for years unchanged
- **!** *"Is it painful or tender, or did it come on with a cold/sore throat?"* — Tender, especially with a recent infection, in a child → reactive lymphadenopathy; painless and firm in an adult → assume malignancy until excluded
- *"Is it firm and fixed, or soft and mobile? Any weight loss, night sweats, or hoarseness?"* — Firm, fixed, with systemic symptoms raises malignancy (nodal, thyroid, or salivary) as the leading concern

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Persistent, unilateral, firm, in an adult** → assume malignancy until excluded
- **Tender, bilateral, in a child or with a recent URTI** → reactive lymphadenopathy

**Red flag:** Persistent unilateral neck lump in an adult → assume malignancy until excluded.

---

### 13. Visual loss — *"Any loss or blurring of vision — one eye or both, sudden or gradual? Any curtain or shadow?"*

**Why we ask:** The tempo and pain split it into emergencies versus chronic causes.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"One eye or both — and does it persist if you close each eye in turn?"* — Confirms it's a true visual field/eye problem, not something that resolves with one eye covered — and localises to one eye vs a field defect
- *"Did it come on suddenly, or gradually over weeks to months?"* — Sudden → emergencies (vascular occlusion, detachment, glaucoma, GCA); gradual → cataract, chronic glaucoma, macular degeneration
- **!** *"Was it like a curtain coming down, and has your vision come back?"* — Curtain descending, then resolving → amaurosis fugax, a TIA warning
- *"Is the eye painful?"* — Pain + visual loss → acute angle-closure glaucoma, optic neuritis, or arteritic causes — a different, more urgent pathway than painless loss
- **!** *"Any flashes of light or floaters beforehand?"* — Flashes and a shower of new floaters → retinal detachment, needs same-day referral
- **!** *"Are you over 50, with any scalp tenderness, jaw pain chewing, or headache on that side?"* — Raises giant cell arteritis — same-day treatment prevents the fellow eye from also being lost
- *"Do you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or atrial fibrillation?"* — Sets pre-test probability for diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, or an embolic retinal artery occlusion
- *"Any weakness, tingling, or bladder/bowel changes alongside it?"* — Raises optic neuritis and multiple sclerosis as a unifying diagnosis

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Sudden painless monocular loss** → retinal artery/vein occlusion, amaurosis fugax (TIA warning — cross-reference neuro), vitreous haemorrhage, retinal detachment (with flashes/floaters/curtain) — emergencies
- **Sudden loss + pain + headache/systemic** → acute angle-closure glaucoma, optic neuritis, GCA (age >50, scalp/jaw symptoms)
- **Gradual painless** → cataract, chronic glaucoma, macular degeneration, refractive/diabetic

**Red flag:** Sudden visual loss is an emergency — same-day ophthalmology.

---

### 14. Red / painful eye — *"Any redness or pain in the eye?"*

**Why we ask:** The painful red eye is sight-threatening until proven otherwise; the benign mimic (conjunctivitis) is gritty and painless with normal vision.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it painful when you move your eyes, and are your eyelids stuck together in the morning?"* — Pain on movement → iritis/scleritis; lids stuck together → conjunctivitis, the benign mimic
- **!** *"Any reduced vision, or sensitivity to light, with it?"* — Reduced vision or photophobia alongside redness makes it sight-threatening — acute glaucoma, uveitis, keratitis, scleritis
- *"Do you wear contact lenses, or have you had an injury or worked with grinding/welding equipment?"* — Raises corneal ulceration, a foreign body, or a flash burn as the cause

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Painful red eye + photophobia/reduced vision** → sight-threatening: acute angle-closure glaucoma, uveitis, keratitis, scleritis
- **Red, gritty, discharge, vision normal, no pain** → conjunctivitis — benign

**Red flag:** Painful red eye with reduced vision needs same-day ophthalmology.

---

### 15. Diplopia — *"Any double vision?"*

**Why we ask:** Diplopia localises a cranial-nerve lesion (III, IV, VI) or raised ICP — cross-reference the Neurological special-senses entry.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Does it go away when you cover one eye?"* — Persists with one eye covered → an eye problem (astigmatism, cataract, dislocated lens), not a nerve palsy; resolves with one eye covered → true binocular diplopia from a cranial nerve or muscle problem
- **!** *"Any drooping eyelid, or is one pupil bigger than the other? Any pain?"* — Painful with a dilated pupil → compressive third nerve palsy (aneurysm), a neurosurgical emergency

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **** → cranial nerve palsy: think microvascular, compressive (aneurysm — painful III with a fixed pupil is an emergency), or raised ICP

---

### 16. Eye discharge — *"Any discharge from the eye?"*

**Why we ask:** The character of the discharge separates the infective causes and flags when it's actually part of a sight-threatening picture rather than simple conjunctivitis.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"What colour is it, and are your eyelids stuck together, especially in the morning?"* — Thick, discoloured, lids stuck together → bacterial; watery with itching/sneezing → allergic; watery after a cold, one eye first → viral
- **!** *"Any pain or reduced vision with the discharge?"* — Pain or reduced vision alongside discharge means this isn't simple conjunctivitis — it's part of a sight-threatening picture

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Watery/mucopurulent, gritty, vision normal** → conjunctivitis (viral or bacterial) — benign
- **Purulent discharge + pain + reduced vision** → part of a sight-threatening picture, not simple conjunctivitis

---

## Quick-recall list (drill version)

EARS: hearing loss (sudden? unilateral?), tinnitus, discharge, earache, vertigo · NOSE: blockage/discharge (unilateral = red flag), epistaxis, smell, facial pain · THROAT: sore throat, dysphagia, hoarseness >3 wks, neck lump · EYES: visual loss (sudden vs gradual, painful vs painless), red/painful eye, diplopia, discharge
