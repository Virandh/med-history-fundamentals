# Reproductive & Sexual History Systems Review

## The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

The reproductive history has one shared trunk and two sex-specific branches. Take the general sexual history first (it applies to everyone and sets the risk context), then branch:

> **GENERAL → FEMALE → MALE**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **General** | Sexual activity & risk | Activity, partners, contraception, pain, fertility, STI risk |
| **Female** | Menstrual, pelvic, endocrine, screening | LMP, cycle, bleeding, discharge, pregnancy, screening |
| **Male** | Genital, functional, screening | Discharge, testicular, erectile function, prostate |

**Framing matters here more than anywhere else.** Signpost why you're asking ("I ask everyone these questions, to make sure I don't miss anything"), use inclusive language, don't assume orientation or partners, and confirm confidentiality. The clinical red flags — ectopic pregnancy, testicular torsion, postmenopausal bleeding — sit inside an intensely personal history, so the manner of asking is part of the skill.

---

## The questions

### 1. Sexual activity & partners — *"I'd like to ask some questions about your sexual health — I ask everyone these. Are you sexually active at the moment? Can you tell me about your partner(s)?"*

**Why we ask:** Frames every reproductive symptom and sets the context for everything that follows. Ask sensitively, without assuming orientation, gender of partners, or number of partners.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Are you currently sexually active, and can you tell me a bit about your partner(s) — gender, number, whether it's one regular partner or more?"* — Establishes the shape of the sexual history without assuming orientation or partner gender — ask open, not leading
- *"Do you have sex with people with a penis, a vagina, or both?"* — Anatomically specific where needed for risk assessment, without relying on labels the patient may not use themselves
- *"When was the last time you had sex, and was it with your regular partner or someone else?"* — Recency and partner type shape the STI risk window more precisely than a general 'sexually active' answer
- *"What type of sexual activity — oral, anal, vaginal?"* — Site of exposure changes which sites need testing and which symptoms are relevant

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Multiple or recent new partners, condomless sex** → raises STI/BBV pre-test probability for everything asked afterward

---

### 2. Contraception & protection — *"What do you use for contraception, if anything? Do you use condoms?"*

**Why we ask:** Contraceptive method affects the differential for abnormal bleeding and the discussion of STI risk — condom use specifically tracks STI/BBV exposure, separately from pregnancy prevention.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"What method are you using, and how long have you been on it?"* — Method and duration matter for the differential of abnormal bleeding — an IUCD, for example, is a recognised cause of irregular bleeding
- *"Do you use condoms as well, or only for contraception?"* — Condom use tracks STI/BBV protection separately from pregnancy prevention — someone can be well protected against one and not the other

---

### 3. Dyspareunia — *"Any pain during sex?"*

**Why we ask:** Deep dyspareunia points to a pelvic cause; superficial pain points to a local/vulval one — the distinction narrows the differential fast.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is the pain deep inside, or more at the entrance?"* — Deep dyspareunia points to a pelvic cause (endometriosis, PID); superficial/entry pain points to a local vulval or vaginal cause
- *"Any pelvic pain, discharge, or dryness alongside it?"* — Pelvic pain alongside deep dyspareunia strengthens endometriosis or PID; dryness with superficial pain points to atrophic vaginitis
- *"Is it new, or has it always been like this?"* — New pain in a previously comfortable pattern points more to an acquired cause than a longstanding structural one

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Deep dyspareunia + pelvic pain** → endometriosis, PID

---

### 4. Fertility — *"Are you currently trying to conceive, or have you had any difficulty with fertility?"*

**Why we ask:** Fertility concerns reframe the relevance of menstrual, endocrine and structural findings, and often prompt the reason for presenting at all.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"How long have you been trying, and how often are you having sex?"* — Over 12 months of trying (or 6 months if over 35) is the threshold that defines infertility and prompts investigation
- *"Any pain during sex, or bleeding between periods?"* — Both raise pelvic pathology (endometriosis, tubal disease) as a contributing cause
- *"Any past pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections, or surgery — for you or your partner?"* — Previous PID or tubal surgery are strong risk factors for tubal-factor infertility; for the partner, ask about mumps orchitis, varicocele, or undescended testes

---

### 5. STI & BBV risk screen — *"Any history of sexually transmitted infections, or contact with someone who has one? Any risk factors like injecting drug use or tattoos done outside a licensed studio?"*

**Why we ask:** Sets pre-test probability for STI symptoms elsewhere in the screen and identifies who should be offered blood-borne virus testing.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Have you ever been diagnosed with an STI, or told a partner had one?"* — Past STI history raises the pre-test probability for a new episode and for tubal damage if untreated
- *"Any injecting drug use, or tattoos or piercings done outside a licensed studio?"* — The classic blood-borne virus exposure risks — worth asking specifically, not assumed
- *"When was your last STI/BBV test, and would you like one today?"* — Normalises testing as routine rather than something only offered when risk seems 'obvious'

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **BBV risk factors** → offer testing (HIV, hepatitis B/C)

---

### 6. Genital rashes, ulcers & lumps — *"Any rashes, ulcers or lumps in the genital area?"*

**Why we ask:** Character and pain of a genital ulcer is a fast bedside split between the common causes.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it painful, or painless?"* — Painful genital ulcer → herpes; painless → think syphilis chancre
- *"Any discharge, and did this start after a new partner?"* — Discharge + dysuria + a new partner points to chlamydia or gonorrhoea
- *"Is there a lump — soft and cystic, or firm?"* — A tender cystic lump near the vaginal entrance suggests a Bartholin's cyst/abscess; firm or ulcerated raises a different, more concerning differential

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Discharge + dysuria + new partner** → STI (chlamydia, gonorrhoea)
- Genital ulcer — painful (herpes) vs painless (syphilis chancre)

---

### 7. Menstrual history — *"When was your last period? Are your cycles regular? How heavy are they?"*

**Why we ask:** The menstrual pattern is a vital sign of reproductive health.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"When was the first day of your last period, and was it normal for you?"* — A light 'period' can actually be implantation bleeding — always ask if it was normal in volume and timing
- *"How many days from the start of one period to the start of the next, and is that regular?"* — Normal cycle length is roughly 21–42 days; a genuine change in pattern is more significant than the absolute numbers
- *"How many days does the bleeding itself last, and how heavy is it — clots, flooding, needing to change protection overnight?"* — Clots, flooding and overnight changes are the practical markers of heavy menstrual bleeding, more reliable than the patient's own estimate of volume
- **!** *"Could you be pregnant?"* — Always establish pregnancy status before attributing a menstrual change to anything else
- **!** *"Any bleeding between periods, after sex, or (if applicable) after menopause?"* — Bleeding outside the expected pattern is the single most important red-flag thread in the whole menstrual history
- *"Any symptoms of thyroid disease, excess hair growth, or milk discharge from the breasts?"* — Secondary amenorrhoea or a cycle disturbance often has an endocrine cause — thyroid, prolactin, or PCOS
- *"Any significant weight change, stress, or intense exercise recently?"* — Functional causes can suppress the cycle just as much as structural or endocrine disease
- *"Are you using any contraception, and could that explain the pattern you're describing?"* — Hormonal contraception and an IUCD are both recognised, common causes of altered bleeding patterns

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Heavy regular periods** → common cause of iron-deficiency anaemia
- **Secondary amenorrhoea** → pregnancy first, then endocrine (thyroid, prolactin, PCOS)

### 8. Dysmenorrhoea — *"Are your periods painful?"*

**Why we ask:** Primary dysmenorrhoea (no pelvic pathology) is common and benign; pain that's new, progressive, or with deep dyspareunia and pelvic pain raises endometriosis.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Did this start in the first year or two after your periods began, or has it developed later?"* — Onset close to menarche, with normal cycles and exam, supports primary dysmenorrhoea; onset years later suggests an acquired, secondary cause
- *"Is the pain getting worse over time, or staying the same?"* — Progressive, worsening pain — especially with deep dyspareunia and pelvic pain — raises endometriosis rather than primary dysmenorrhoea
- *"Does anything else come with it — nausea, vomiting, or looser bowel motions?"* — These accompany primary dysmenorrhoea too, so their presence doesn't confirm or exclude it — but their absence alongside progressive pain adds weight to a secondary cause

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Progressive, severe dysmenorrhoea + deep dyspareunia + pelvic pain** → endometriosis

---

### 9. Abnormal bleeding — *"Any bleeding between periods, after sex, or after menopause?"*

**Why we ask:** Bleeding outside the expected pattern is the single most important menstrual red-flag thread — each pattern points to a different structural cause.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is this between periods, after sex, or after you'd stopped having periods for good?"* — Each pattern points to a different structural cause and carries different urgency
- **!** *"If postmenopausal — how long had your periods stopped before this happened?"* — Postmenopausal bleeding is endometrial cancer until excluded, regardless of how light it is
- **!** *"Is it after sex specifically?"* — Postcoital bleeding points to cervical pathology — ectropion, cervicitis, or cervical cancer — check the screening history
- *"Any pelvic pain, discharge, or fever with it?"* — Suggests an infective or inflammatory cause alongside the bleeding, rather than a purely structural one
- **!** *"Could you be pregnant?"* — Pregnancy must be excluded before anything else in a reproductive-age woman with abnormal bleeding
- *"Are you up to date with cervical screening — do you know the result of your last test?"* — A missed or abnormal screening history changes the urgency of investigating postcoital or intermenstrual bleeding
- *"Are you on any hormonal contraception, or do you have an IUCD?"* — Both are common, usually benign explanations for irregular bleeding — but don't let that stop you asking the red-flag questions first
- *"Any risk factors for endometrial cancer — diabetes, high blood pressure, obesity, or PCOS?"* — These conditions raise the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and malignancy, adding weight to a postmenopausal or intermenstrual bleeding presentation

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Postmenopausal bleeding** → endometrial cancer until excluded
- **Postcoital bleeding** → cervical pathology (ectropion, cervicitis, cervical cancer) — check screening history
- **Intermenstrual bleeding** → cervical or endometrial pathology — an indication for investigation (hysteroscopy ± endometrial biopsy)

**Red flag:** Postmenopausal bleeding is endometrial cancer until excluded.

---

### 10. Pelvic pain & discharge — *"Any pelvic pain or vaginal discharge?"*

**Why we ask:** Discharge character and associated pain/fever separate the infective causes.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Where exactly is the pain, and did it come on suddenly or build up gradually?"* — Sudden, severe, one-sided pain raises ovarian torsion or ectopic pregnancy; gradual onset with discharge points more to PID
- *"Any fever, or pain that's worse with sex or when you move?"* — Fever with pain and discharge → PID; cervical motion tenderness is the clinical hallmark, though examination is needed to elicit it
- *"What does the discharge look like — colour, smell, and is it itchy?"* — Thick white and itchy → candida; thin grey with a fishy smell, minimal itch → bacterial vaginosis; frothy yellow-green and itchy → trichomonas
- **!** *"Could you be pregnant?"* — Pregnancy must be excluded in any reproductive-age woman with acute pelvic pain — ectopic and other pregnancy-related causes come first

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Discharge + pain + fever** → PID; itchy discharge → candida (bacterial vaginosis is characteristically non-itchy — its clue is a thin grey, fishy-smelling discharge, not pruritus)
- **Reproductive-age woman with acute pelvic pain** → exclude pregnancy/ectopic first

---

### 11. Pregnancy status & obstetric history — *"Is there any chance you could be pregnant? Tell me about any previous pregnancies."*

**Why we ask:** The possibility of pregnancy reframes any abdominal pain and must be established before imaging or prescribing.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is there any chance you could be pregnant — when was your last period, and are you sexually active?"* — Establishes pregnancy status before anything else is assumed, investigated, or prescribed
- **!** *"Any one-sided abdominal pain with the bleeding or missed period?"* — Sudden unilateral pain + a missed period + bleeding is the classic (though inconsistently complete) triad of ectopic pregnancy — a can't-miss
- *"Have you had any previous pregnancies, miscarriages, ectopic pregnancies, or terminations?"* — A previous ectopic pregnancy or pelvic infection substantially raises the risk of another ectopic pregnancy
- **!** *"Any shoulder-tip pain, dizziness, or feeling like you might faint?"* — Shoulder-tip pain from diaphragmatic irritation, with dizziness or faintness, suggests a ruptured ectopic bleeding into the peritoneum — a surgical emergency

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Abdominal pain + missed period + possible pregnancy** → ectopic pregnancy until excluded (a can't-miss)
- Always establish pregnancy status before imaging or prescribing
- **Shoulder-tip pain, dizziness or fainting alongside suspected ectopic pregnancy** → possible rupture and intraperitoneal bleeding, a surgical emergency

**Red flag:** Abdominal pain + missed period → ectopic pregnancy until excluded.

---

### 12. Galactorrhoea, hyperandrogenism & menopause — *"Any milk discharge from the breasts, excess hair or acne, or menopausal symptoms?"*

**Why we ask:** Links reproductive symptoms to the endocrine axes — each pattern points to a distinct hormonal cause.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is the milk discharge spontaneous, or only when you squeeze the nipple? One side or both?"* — Spontaneous, uniduct, bloodstained discharge is more concerning than an expressed, multiduct, or bilateral discharge, which is usually physiological
- *"Any change in your periods or libido alongside the discharge, excess hair, or acne?"* — Ties the finding back to its likely axis — prolactin excess suppresses periods and libido; hyperandrogenism with irregular cycles points to PCOS
- *"Any hot flushes, night sweats, or mood changes, and have your periods stopped for 12 months or more?"* — Twelve months of amenorrhoea is the definition of menopause — vasomotor symptoms support it further

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Nipple discharge / galactorrhoea** → prolactin (cross-reference pituitary)
- **Hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne)** → PCOS
- **Menopausal symptoms (hot flushes, mood swings, night sweats)** → menopause

---

### 13. Cervical & breast screening — *"Are you up to date with cervical and breast screening?"*

**Why we ask:** The two population cancer-screening programs — easy to skip if you're focused on the presenting symptom, but part of a complete reproductive history.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"When was your last cervical screening test, and do you know the result?"* — An overdue or previously abnormal result changes the urgency of investigating any bleeding symptom
- *"Do you do regular breast checks, and has anyone in your family had breast or ovarian cancer, especially at a young age?"* — A first-degree relative with breast cancer, or known BRCA status, changes the screening schedule and threshold for investigating any breast symptom

---

### 14. Urethral discharge — *"Any discharge from the penis, or rashes or ulcers?"*

**Why we ask:** Urethral discharge is the cardinal STI symptom in men.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"What colour is it, and is there any pain or burning when you pass urine?"* — Discharge with dysuria is the cardinal STI pattern in men — chlamydia and gonorrhoea are the leading causes
- *"When did this start relative to your last sexual contact?"* — Timing helps distinguish gonorrhoea (usually days) from chlamydia (often longer incubation, sometimes asymptomatic)
- *"Any rash or ulcers on the penis, or joint pains or red eyes?"* — Screens for a concurrent genital ulcer and for reactive arthritis, which can follow a urethral STI

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Urethral discharge** → STI screen

---

### 15. Testicular pain / lump — *"Any testicular pain, lumps or swelling?"*

**Why we ask:** Testicular symptoms host two can't-miss diagnoses at opposite ends of the tempo spectrum — a painless lump and a painful acute scrotum.

**If yes, ask:**
- **!** *"Did the pain come on suddenly and severely, or gradually?"* — Sudden, severe pain in a young man is testicular torsion until excluded — a surgical emergency measured in hours
- **!** *"Is it a lump, and is it painful or painless?"* — A painless lump is testicular cancer until excluded; a painful swelling points more to infection (epididymo-orchitis)
- *"Any discharge, burning on urination, or fever with it?"* — Supports epididymo-orchitis, usually from an ascending infection or STI, rather than torsion or a tumour
- *"Any recent injury, or has this happened before and settled on its own?"* — Recurrent, self-settling pain can suggest intermittent torsion — still worth urgent assessment, not reassurance

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Painless testicular lump** → testicular cancer until excluded
- **Painful swelling + dysuria/discharge** → epididymo-orchitis

**Red flag:** Sudden severe testicular pain in a young man → testicular torsion (surgical emergency).

---

### 16. Erectile function — *"Any difficulty with erections?"*

**Why we ask:** Erectile dysfunction is an early vascular marker — it shares risk factors with IHD and often predates a cardiac event — and a common drug/endocrine effect.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Is it difficulty getting an erection, keeping one, or both — and has this been going on for at least a few months?"* — Persistent difficulty over roughly three months is what defines erectile dysfunction rather than an occasional, situational episode
- *"Do you still get erections at other times — waking up in the morning, for example?"* — Preserved nocturnal/morning erections point to a psychological cause; their loss points more to an organic (vascular, neurological, hormonal) one
- *"Any chest pain, breathlessness, or known heart or vascular disease? What medications are you on?"* — ED shares risk factors with IHD and can predate a cardiac event — review antihypertensives, antidepressants and mood alongside the vascular screen

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **ED + cardiovascular risk factors** → endothelial/vascular disease; review antihypertensives, antidepressants, and mood

---

### 17. Prostate screening — *"Any urinary symptoms, or have you discussed prostate checks?"*

**Why we ask:** Prostate disease presents through the voiding LUTS screen (cross-reference GU) as much as through a screening conversation.

**If yes, ask:**
- *"Any trouble starting, a weak stream, or needing to go more often, especially at night?"* — Voiding LUTS are how prostate disease usually first presents — cross-reference the full GU voiding-symptoms work-up
- *"Any family history of prostate cancer, and have you discussed screening with your doctor?"* — Family history shifts the risk-benefit conversation about PSA testing, which should be a shared decision, not a routine reflex

**Rules in / rules out:**
- Prostate: voiding LUTS (cross-reference GU), family history, screening discussion

## Quick-recall list (drill version)

**GENERAL:** active?, partners (gender/number/type), contraception/condoms, dyspareunia, fertility, STI contact, BBV risk (IVDU/tattoos), genital lesions · **FEMALE:** LMP + cycle + volume, dysmenorrhoea, abnormal bleeding (postmenopausal/postcoital/intermenstrual), pelvic pain + discharge, pregnancy status + obstetric history, galactorrhoea/hyperandrogenism/menopause, cervical + breast screening · **MALE:** urethral discharge, testicular pain/lump, erectile function, prostate screening
