# Foundations of History Taking

The reasoning behind history taking itself — the shape of every consultation, the tool for dissecting any symptom, and the risk screen that meets the patient as a person. Master these three and every organ-system screen slots into place.


## 1. The Clinical History — Structure

### The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

Every history — no matter the presenting problem — hangs on the same skeleton. Learn the skeleton once and you can rebuild any history under pressure, then flex the detail to fit the patient. The order below is Talley’s: let the patient tell the story first (open), then take control and fill the gaps (directed).

> **GREET → COMPLAINT → HPI → PAST → DRUGS → FAMILY → SOCIAL → REVIEW → ICE**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Greet & open** | Rapport, then the story in the patient’s words | Introduce & consent; open question; the "golden minute"; "What else?" |
| **Complaint** | The principal symptom that brought them in | What concerns you most? (patient’s own words, no jargon) |
| **HPI** | Characterise each complaint fully | SOCRATES + the 4 diagnostic questions (Where/What/How/Why) |
| **Past history** | Prior illness, surgery, obstetric, immunisation | Serious illnesses, operations, admissions, transfusions |
| **Drugs & allergies** | Current/past meds + TRUE allergies | Dose, duration, indication, adherence; OTC & complementary |
| **Family history** | Heritable & familial risk | Similar illness in relatives; ages/causes of death |
| **Social history** | The person, their risks and supports | Smoking, alcohol, drugs, occupation, home (see Social entry) |
| **Systems review** | Screen the other systems for missed disease | Pertinent positives AND negatives (see the 13 system screens) |
| **ICE & close** | The patient’s perspective and the effect of illness | Ideas, Concerns, Expectations; "Is there anything else?" |

Note the two halves: the patient leads the **first** half (open question, uninterrupted story — the average doctor interrupts inside ~20 seconds; give it closer to a minute), and the clinician leads the **second** half (specific questions to test hypotheses). Record the presenting complaint in the patient’s own words.

> **See also** — SOCRATES — Analysing Any Symptom (the tool for the HPI); Social History & Risk Screen (the SOCIAL block expanded); and the 13 system screens (the SYSTEMS REVIEW block).

---

### The questions

#### 1. Opening & rapport — *"Introduce yourself and your role, gain consent, then: "What has been the trouble recently?" or "When were you last quite well?""*

**Why we ask:** Rapport is the substrate of an accurate history — patients disclose more when at ease. Open the interview with an open-ended question and then listen: as Osler put it, the patient will "tell you the diagnosis" if you let them. Avoid "What brought you in?" (invites "an ambulance").

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Let the patient talk uninterrupted first** → the "golden minute"; premature closure is the commonest interviewing error
- **"Is there anything else?" / "What else?"** → reopens the story and surfaces the symptom they were building up to
- **Deep personal/social questions up front** → can feel intrusive; defer until you’ve shown interest in the presenting problem

---

#### 2. Presenting complaint — *"Of all these, which problem is troubling you the most?"*

**Why we ask:** A patient often has several symptoms; you must decide which one led them to present — and the patient’s idea of "serious" may differ from yours (the person with a cold who mentions, in passing, coughing up blood needs attention on the chest, not the nose). Record it in the patient’s own words, no technical terms yet.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **The symptom that most concerns the patient** → must be addressed, even if minor, or they leave dissatisfied
- **A symptom mentioned "in passing" (e.g. haemoptysis)** → may be the most important — don’t let the loudest complaint bury it

---

#### 3. History of the presenting illness — *"Tell me about it from the very beginning — when did you last feel completely well?"*

**Why we ask:** The core of the history. Get the story in chronological order, one problem at a time. Characterise each symptom with SOCRATES, and frame every complaint against the four diagnostic questions: Where (anatomical), What (pathological), How (functional), Why (aetiological) — a diagnosis is a disease process, not just a name.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Where is it?** → anatomical diagnosis · What is its nature? → pathological diagnosis
- **How does it affect you?** → functional diagnosis · Why did it develop? → aetiological diagnosis
- **Finish one symptom before moving to the next** → politely hold the garrulous patient: "I’d like to finish about your blackouts before we discuss the tiredness."

---

#### 4. Past medical & surgical history — *"Have you had any serious illnesses, operations, or hospital admissions? Any childhood illnesses? Are your vaccinations up to date?"*

**Why we ask:** Past illness shapes current risk and constrains investigation. Preface it ("These may affect your current tests and treatment") as it can feel intrusive. Some operations carry continuing risk (valve surgery, cancer surgery, prostheses); some conditions contraindicate tests.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Prostheses / pacemakers** → pacemaker = MRI contraindication; foreign body = infection risk
- **CKD** → caution with iodinated contrast and gadolinium; pregnancy → avoid radiation
- **A "diagnosis" the patient reports** → verify it (untested "ulcer" may never have been one); keep objective scepticism

---

#### 5. Drug history & allergies — *"What tablets, inhalers, patches or medicines are you on — can you show me them all? Do you take anything from the chemist or any natural remedies? Any allergies?"*

**Why we ask:** The drug list is a map of hidden diagnoses (an antihypertensive reveals forgotten "normal" blood pressure). Ask by drug class to jog memory, and about OTC/complementary agents (~50% use them; some adulterated). Aspirin/NSAIDs — but not paracetamol — cause GI bleeding.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Prompt by class** → BP, cholesterol, diabetes, arthritis, mood, contraception (many don’t count "the pill"), HRT, epilepsy, anticoagulation
- Capture dose, duration, indication, adherence, side effects for each

**Red flag:** always separate a TRUE allergy (rash, bronchospasm) from a side effect — patients routinely confuse them, and a mislabelled "allergy" narrows future treatment unnecessarily. Ask exactly what the reaction was.

---

#### 6. Family history — *"Do any illnesses run in your family? Are your parents and siblings well — if not, what did they have, and at what age?"*

**Why we ask:** Familial clustering flags both inherited disease and shared risk. Premature IHD in a first-degree relative is a major risk factor; bowel and breast cancer are the two malignancies patients report accurately. Draw a family tree if a heritable disorder is suspected.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Numerous affected relatives, earlier-than-usual onset, the less-often-affected sex, or disease despite no risk factors** → raised genetic risk
- **Consanguinity** → increases autosomal-recessive risk — ask tactfully if the pedigree suggests it

---

#### 7. Systems review — *"A quick run through the other systems — have you had any [targeted screen of head-to-toe symptoms]?"*

**Why we ask:** The safety net: a brief screen of the systems you haven’t covered, to catch important disease the presenting complaint would otherwise miss. Experienced clinicians do a TARGETED review guided by what they’ve already heard — not every question on every patient.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Record pertinent NEGATIVES, not just positives** → they narrow the differential and document your reasoning
- **A new symptom unmasked here** → stop and characterise it fully, then fold it back into the HPI

---

#### 8. ICE & the effect of illness — *"What do you think might be going on? Is there anything you’re particularly worried about? What were you hoping we could do? — and how has this affected your life?"*

**Why we ask:** Before closing, elicit the patient’s Ideas, Concerns and Expectations, and the illness’s functional/psychological impact. Their chief worry may not be the worst symptom but a specific fear — addressing it is central to rapport and to a management plan they’ll follow.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Ask what they think is wrong and most fear** → often reframes the consultation entirely
- **A serious illness reshapes work, finances, relationships and self-esteem** → ask how they’re coping and what they hope for

---

### Discriminator table: the four diagnostic questions (what every complaint should answer)

| Question | Diagnosis it pins down | Worked example (chest pain) |
|---|---|---|
| Where is it? | Anatomical | Retrosternal → cardiac/oesophageal, not chest wall |
| What is its nature? | Pathological | Crushing/tight → ischaemia; tearing → dissection |
| How does it affect you? | Functional | Stops you walking to the letterbox → severity & disability |
| Why did it develop? | Aetiological | Smoker, diabetic, family history → coronary risk |

### Quick-recall list (drill version)

**GREET:** introduce, consent, open question, golden minute · **COMPLAINT:** principal symptom in own words · **HPI:** SOCRATES + Where/What/How/Why, chronological · **PAST:** illnesses, operations, transfusions, immunisations · **DRUGS:** dose/duration/indication/adherence + OTC + TRUE allergy vs side effect · **FAMILY:** similar illness, ages/causes of death · **SOCIAL:** smoking, alcohol, drugs, occupation, home · **REVIEW:** targeted, pertinent negatives · **ICE:** ideas, concerns, expectations + effect of illness


## 2. SOCRATES — Analysing Any Symptom

### The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

SOCRATES is the tool that turns a vague complaint into a characterised symptom you can reason about. It works for ANY symptom (not just pain) — Radiation is the one element reserved for pain or discomfort. Run every presenting symptom through it in the HPI.

> **S ite · O nset · C haracter · R adiation · A ssociated · T iming · E xacerbating/relieving · S everity**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **S — Site** | Where it is & whether localised | "Point to exactly where." Localised vs diffuse |
| **O — Onset** | How & how fast it began | Sudden vs gradual; what were you doing at the time? |
| **C — Character** | What the symptom actually is | Sharp/dull/burning/cramping? "What do you mean by …?" |
| **R — Radiation** | Where pain travels (pain only) | Does it move or spread anywhere? |
| **A — Associated** | The accompanying cluster | Symptoms of the same system — the combination diagnoses |
| **T — Timing** | Course over time | When did it start? Constant vs intermittent? Why present now? |
| **E — Exac./reliev.** | What modifies it | What makes it better or worse? |
| **S — Severity** | How bad / how disabling | Does it stop you doing things? Graded mild→very severe |

The power is in the pattern, not any single answer: it is usually the COMBINATION of characteristic features — not one symptom — that reliably suggests the diagnosis. Onset and the exacerbating/relieving pair are often the most discriminating letters.

> **See also** — The Clinical History — Structure (SOCRATES sits inside the HPI); Cardiovascular (chest pain is the classic SOCRATES worked example).

---

### The questions

#### 1. Site — *"Where exactly is it? Can you point to the spot?"*

**Why we ask:** Localisation narrows the anatomy. Ask the patient to point — and note that some symptoms deliberately don’t localise (dizziness, cough, breathlessness, weight change), which is itself informative.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Well-localised, patient points with one finger** → somatic/parietal source (e.g. localised peritonitis)
- **Diffuse, can’t pinpoint** → visceral or non-localised symptom (early appendicitis is vague periumbilical before it localises)

---

#### 2. Onset — *"How did it start — suddenly or gradually? What were you doing at that moment?"*

**Why we ask:** Mode of onset is one of the most discriminating questions in medicine. Instantaneous onset and offset points to arrhythmia; a sudden severe "thunderclap" points to a catastrophe; what the patient was doing at onset can hand you the diagnosis.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Instantaneous on AND off** → cardiac arrhythmia
- **Breathlessness that WAKES them from sleep** → cardiac failure (PND)
- **Sudden loss of consciousness with immediate full recovery** → cardiac (arrhythmia), not neurological

**Red flag:** a sudden, severe "worst-ever" headache reaching peak in seconds (thunderclap) → subarachnoid haemorrhage until proven otherwise.

---

#### 3. Character — *"What is it actually like? If you had to describe the feeling…"*

**Why we ask:** Force the patient to define the symptom in their own terms, because lay words hide different meanings. "Dizziness" may be vertigo (room spinning) or presyncope; "indigestion" may mean pain, heartburn, fullness, wind or altered bowel habit. Pin it down: "What do you mean by …?"

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Vertigo (room spins)** → vestibular/neurological; light-headed "about to faint" → presyncope/cardiovascular
- **Chest pain described as "sharp"** → makes angina LESS likely (ischaemic pain is typically heavy/tight/dull)

---

#### 4. Radiation — *"Does the pain travel or spread anywhere else?"*

**Why we ask:** Applies mainly to pain. Certain radiation patterns are characteristic or even diagnostic — the pattern of spread points to the source or the nerve territory involved.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Pain in a dermatomal (nerve-root) band** → e.g. herpes zoster / radiculopathy
- **Central chest pain radiating to jaw or arm** → supports cardiac ischaemia; to the back, tearing → dissection

---

#### 5. Associated symptoms — *"Have you noticed anything else that comes with it?"*

**Why we ask:** Systematically uncover the symptoms expected with disease of that region — give first and fullest attention to the system of the presenting complaint. A single symptom may be the clue, but the characteristic CLUSTER is what reliably makes the diagnosis.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Chest pain + sweating + nausea + breathlessness** → the ischaemic cluster
- **Headache + fever + neck stiffness + photophobia** → the meningitic cluster

---

#### 6. Timing — *"When did it first begin? Is it there all the time or does it come and go? Why have you come about it now?"*

**Why we ask:** Date the onset as precisely as you can — "what was the first thing you noticed was wrong?" and "when did you last feel entirely well?" Establish continuous vs intermittent, and in long-standing symptoms ask why they’re presenting today (a change usually prompts it).

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Intermittent, stereotyped, fully resolves between** → paroxysmal process (arrhythmia, migraine, TIA)
- **Progressive, never fully clears** → structural/chronic process
- **Ask about prior similar episodes** → recurrence pattern is diagnostic in itself

---

#### 7. Exacerbating & relieving factors — *"Does anything make it better or worse?"*

**Why we ask:** Modifiers are frequently the most specific clue to mechanism. What relieves or provokes a symptom often localises the organ and the pathology.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Relieved by sitting forward** → pericarditis; relieved by antacid/milk → acid reflux
- **Worse on the slightest movement** → peritonitis; worse on movement/weight-bearing in the big toe → gout
- **Brought on by exertion, relieved by rest** → angina (or claudication in the legs)

---

#### 8. Severity — *"How bad is it — does it stop you doing things, or wake you from sleep?"*

**Why we ask:** Severity is subjective; the best anchor is FUNCTIONAL impact, not a bare number. Grade mild (ignorable) → moderate (can’t be ignored) → severe (limits daily activity) → very severe. Quantify concretely where you can (metres walked before breathless; rest vs exertional angina).

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Grade by interference with activity/sleep** → more reproducible than "out of 10"
- **Concrete quantification** → SOB after 10 m on the flat is worse than after 90 m uphill; angina at rest is more significant than on exertion
- The 0–10 scale is better for tracking change over time than absolute severity — and demanding a number from someone in severe pain can be unkind

---

### Discriminator table: onset & pattern — what the mode of onset signals

| Onset / pattern | Points to | Why it discriminates |
|---|---|---|
| Instantaneous on & off | Arrhythmia | Electrical events start and stop abruptly |
| Thunderclap (secs to peak) | Subarachnoid haemorrhage | Sudden arterial rupture — red flag |
| Wakes from sleep breathless | Cardiac failure (PND) | Recumbent fluid redistribution |
| Sudden LOC, instant recovery | Cardiac (arrhythmia) | Neurological causes recover slowly |
| Gradual, less exertion over months | Chronic structural (e.g. COPD) | Progressive loss of reserve |

### Quick-recall list (drill version)

**S**ite (point to it; localised vs diffuse) · **O**nset (sudden vs gradual; doing what?) · **C**haracter (sharp/dull/burning; "what do you mean?") · **R**adiation (does it travel? — pain only) · **A**ssociated (the cluster) · **T**iming (when began, constant vs intermittent, why now) · **E**xacerbating/relieving (better/worse) · **S**everity (functional impact; mild→very severe)


## 3. Social History & Risk Screen

### The scaffold (how to reconstruct this list under pressure)

The social history is where you meet the patient as a person — and where much of the modifiable risk and the discharge-planning reality lives. Ask it conversationally, not as a rote checklist. It carries the highest-yield named screening tools (WHACS, pack-years, CAGE/AUDIT-C, the single-question drug screen).

> **HOME → OCCUPATION → SMOKING → ALCOHOL → DRUGS → LIFESTYLE → MOOD & INTIMACY**

| Chunk | What it probes | Questions it generates |
|---|---|---|
| **Home** | Living situation, support, function | Who’s at home? Managing? Caregiver? Steps/toilet? Driving? |
| **Occupation** | Exposures & job-linked disease | WHACS: What / How / Anything concerning / Colleagues / Satisfied |
| **Smoking** | Tobacco & vaping burden | Ever smoked? How many/day × how many years → pack-years |
| **Alcohol** | Quantity + dependence screen | Type, how much, how often; CAGE / AUDIT-C |
| **Drugs** | Recreational & injecting use | Single-question screen; IV use & needle sharing |
| **Lifestyle** | Diet, exercise, travel | Diet adequacy, activity level, recent overseas travel |
| **Mood & intimacy** | Psychosocial & sexual | Mood/anhedonia screen; sexual history if relevant |

Match depth to relevance and timing: living situation and function matter most in chronic/disabling illness; the intrusive questions (sexual history, drug use, mood) land better once rapport is built — sometimes at a later consultation. Use the social history as the natural moment for smoking-cessation and safe-drinking advice.

> **See also** — The Clinical History — Structure (the SOCIAL block in context); Psychiatric (mood, anhedonia, risk); Reproductive & Sexual History (the detailed sexual history).

---

### The questions

#### 1. Home, support & function — *"Who’s at home with you? Are you managing day to day on your own? How do you get to the shops?"*

**Why we ask:** Determines whether the person can safely function — the pivot of any discharge or management plan in chronic or disabling illness. Establish the main caregiver, the physical setup (steps to the door, location of the toilet), and mobility.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Can’t manage ADLs unaided** → identify the main caregiver and available social support early
- **Physical barriers (steps, upstairs toilet)** → shape rehab and discharge planning

---

#### 2. Occupation & hobbies — *"What do you do for work? What does that involve day to day? — (WHACS)"*

**Why we ask:** Occupation and hobbies link directly to disease. The WHACS mnemonic structures it: What do you do? How do you do it? Anything you’re concerned about (exposures)? Colleagues affected? Satisfied with the job? Ask specifically about dusts, chemicals and biological exposures.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Dust/mineral exposure** → asbestosis (builders, shipyards), silicosis (stonemasons, miners), pneumoconiosis (coal)
- **Organic/biological exposure** → hypersensitivity pneumonitis (farmers’ mouldy hay, bird fanciers), occupational asthma (timber)
- **Ask if fellow workers are affected** → shared exposure supports an occupational cause

---

#### 3. Smoking & vaping — *"Have you ever smoked? How many a day, and for how many years? Have you stopped — when? Do you vape?"*

**Why we ask:** Never ask only "do you smoke?" (a patient who quit this morning says no). Quantify as PACK-YEARS — (cigarettes/day ÷ 20) × years — because risk scales with cumulative dose and recency. Ask about vaping separately (nicotine content, EVALI risk).

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **1 pack-year = 20 cigarettes/day for 1 year** → the standard unit of tobacco burden
- **Smoking** → premature coronary & peripheral vascular disease, COPD, many cancers, peptic ulcer, fetal harm
- **Modern packs hold 30–50, not 20** → clarify actual number to compute pack-years correctly

---

#### 4. Alcohol — *"Do you drink alcohol? Beer, wine or spirits — and how many glasses on most days? — then screen (CAGE / AUDIT-C)."*

**Why we ask:** Quantify first (1 standard drink ≈ 8–10 g ethanol), then screen for a use disorder. Self-report is often an underestimate — hold it with gentle scepticism. Liver risk rises above ~80 g/day (men) or ~40 g/day (women) sustained for years.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **CAGE ≥ 2 "yes"** → suggests dependence (Cut down / Annoyed / Guilty / Eye-opener; 77% sens, 79% spec)
- **AUDIT-C ≥ 3 (women) / ≥ 4 (men)** → unhealthy use — more sensitive than CAGE for excess drinking short of dependence
- **Excess alcohol** → look for its complications: liver disease, pancreatitis, GI bleeding, cardiomyopathy/AF, withdrawal, neuropathy

**Red flag:** flag the guideline nuance: Talley gives BOTH CAGE and AUDIT-C but notes CAGE misses unhealthy drinking short of dependence, so AUDIT-C (or the single "how many times in the past year 5+/4+ drinks?" question) is preferred for screening. Current NHMRC (AU) safe limits: ≤ 10 standard drinks/week AND ≤ 4 on any one day.

---

#### 5. Recreational & injecting drugs — *"How many times in the past year have you used a recreational drug, or a prescription medication for non-medical reasons?"*

**Why we ask:** That single question is ~100% sensitive (74% specific) for problem drug use — an efficient, non-judgemental opener. Injecting use adds blood-borne-virus and endocarditis risk; ask about needle sharing.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Any non-zero answer** → explore substances, route, frequency, and dependence
- **IV use** → HIV/hepatitis B & C, right-sided endocarditis, injection-site infection; ask about needle sharing
- Judge relevance — no need to interrogate the 90-year-old nursing-home resident

---

#### 6. Diet, exercise & travel — *"Tell me briefly about your diet and how physically active you are. Have you travelled overseas recently?"*

**Why we ask:** Lifestyle modifies cardiometabolic risk; travel reframes infection risk. For diet, note adequacy and who cooks (frailty/social clue). For travel, ask destinations, how they lived (water, food), any illness or hospitalisation abroad, and malaria prophylaxis.

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Recent travel + fever** → ask destination, exposures, prophylaxis (malaria), and overseas hospitalisation (resistant organisms)
- **Poor diet / can’t cook / relies on others** → nutritional and functional red flag in the frail or elderly

---

#### 7. Mood & sexual history — *"How are things going at home and at work? Have you been feeling down, or lost interest in things you normally enjoy? — and, where relevant, a sexual history."*

**Why we ask:** Depression is common (prevalence up to ~8%) and increases threefold with any significant medical illness — screen with mood + anhedonia; a "no" to both makes major depression unlikely. The sexual history is asked only when relevant and with good timing (see Reproductive).

**Rules in / rules out:**
- **Low mood AND anhedonia** → screen further; if depression likely, ask about suicidal ideation (asking does NOT increase risk)
- **Organic mimics of depression** → hypothyroidism, Cushing’s — keep them in mind
- Defer intrusive sexual/personal questions until rapport is established — good judgement on timing matters

---

### Discriminator table: alcohol screening tools — which to reach for

| Tool | What it is | Positive / note |
|---|---|---|
| CAGE | 4 yes/no: Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, Eye-opener | ≥ 2 "yes" → likely dependence (77%/79%); misses unhealthy use |
| AUDIT-C | 3 Qs: frequency, quantity, binges (0–4 each) | ≥ 3 women / ≥ 4 men → unhealthy use; better sensitivity |
| Single question | "Times in past year with 5+ (men) / 4+ (women) drinks?" | > 0 (or "can’t remember") → positive; nearly as good as AUDIT-C |

### Quick-recall list (drill version)

**HOME:** who’s home, support, ADLs, caregiver, access/driving · **OCCUPATION:** WHACS + specific exposures (dust/chemical/biological) · **SMOKING:** pack-years (/day÷20×years) + vaping · **ALCOHOL:** quantify (std drinks) + CAGE/AUDIT-C + NHMRC ≤10/wk, ≤4/day · **DRUGS:** single-question screen; IV → BBV/endocarditis · **LIFESTYLE:** diet, exercise, travel (+ prophylaxis) · **MOOD & INTIMACY:** mood + anhedonia (± suicide risk), sexual history if relevant
